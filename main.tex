\begin{abstract}
\noindent To study the spread of misinformation on digital platforms, it is imperative for researchers to have access to reliable and valid tools for identifying cases of false information. Here, we describe different approaches to select misinformation, for instance, by using either fact-checked news stories or a list of web domains of news sources. Further, we analyze how the choice of measurement influences downstream research results and use the NewsGuard database as a case study. The organization NewsGuard offers the most comprehensive database of source trustworthiness ratings and is increasing in popularity despite restrictive access. Yet, the database was neither created as a research instrument nor has it ever been reviewed systematically for this purpose. Therefore, we provide a comprehensive description and inspection of the NewsGuard database across the stability of trustworthiness ratings over time, the selection of sources, and the completeness of additionally rated news categories, such as political orientation. By describing the database over time and across countries, we outline potential pitfalls and recommendations for digital media research and discuss appropriate use cases. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Misinformation permeates both social media platforms \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018} and mainstream media channels \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. 
However, only a fraction of news consumption is attributed to false news -- roughly ~0.15\% \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020} -- and only a small group of users actively engage with unreliable information online \citep{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. 
Typically, these individuals hold strong political or ideological beliefs and accept and actively look for information that echoes their opinions \citep{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022} -- information that often comes from political elites or established media sources \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. 
Consequently, it is unsurprising that misinformation capitalizes on these dynamics to stoke conflict and evoke negative emotions, thereby exacerbating partisan divides and fueling inter-group hostility \citep{gonzalez-bailonAsymmetricIdeologicalSegregation2023a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. 
Against a backdrop of declining trust in both media outlets \citep{newmanReutersInstituteDigital2023} and institutions \citep{bennettDisinformationAge2020}, it becomes increasingly crucial to comprehend the mechanisms driving the dissemination of misinformation, particularly in the digital sphere. 
Digital media not only facilitate the measurement of these dynamics but also have the potential to reinforce polarization and misinformation \citep{lorenz-spreenSystematicReviewWorldwide2022}.

Scholars across scientific disciplines have extensively studied different aspects of misinformation, therefore relying on various conceptualizations. Conceptual differences in the boundaries and harmfulness of misinformation affect the measurement of the concept, while methodological constraints influence the definition. Terms like misinformation, disinformation, fake news, rumors, or conspiracy theories are often used interchangeably \cite{guessMisinformationDisinformationOnline2020}, yet they share a common focus on facticity.
In communication science, a popular definition by Wardle and Derakhshan (\citeyear{wardleInformationDisorderInterdisciplinary2017}) differentiates between unintentional misinformation and deliberate disinformation designed with the intent to misinform. Here, disinformation includes fake news as a type of disinformation that pursues financial or political intentions. According to this definition, misinformation covers only false and misleading information that is created unintentionally, such as honest mistakes or incomplete reporting. Such nuances are important when researching, for instance, the content of misinformation or the diffusion of specific fake news articles. Computational social scientists, such as Lazer and colleagues (\citeyear{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}), tend to follow this notion of information disorders:
"Fake news overlaps with other information disorders, such as misinformation (false or misleading information) and disinformation (false information that is purposely spread to deceive people)." (p. 1094) 
In practice, however, it is difficult to determine the exact intention, especially in research contexts that focus on effects\citep{weeksWhatNextSix2021}. In psychology, researchers, therefore, commonly use misinformation as an umbrella term for inaccurate or unreliable information, regardless of intent, e.g., \cite{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022} or \cite{vanderlindenMisinformationSusceptibilitySpread2022}. 

The ambiguous nature of misinformation and the ethical challenge of who defines misinformation present researchers with numerous methodological challenges, leaving them with a number of degrees of freedom. 
Defining the boundaries of misinformation, essentially deciding where to draw the line to the truth, is a very complex task for numerous reasons: 
First, misinformation often appears in subtle forms rather than obvious lies \citep{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a, bakirFakeNewsEconomy2018a, vargoAgendasettingPowerFake2018}, making it hard to define where misinformation starts. In the real world, professional editors and politicians leave out true but important details, or they decontextualize parts to fit a narrative or a storyline without saying anything wrong. Implicit interpretations of facts can be different from explicit lying, making subtle forms of misinformation hard to detect, especially using automated methods (REF Noelle).
Second, making this decision involves a number of ethical issues, and different groups of people would come to different conclusions. Trying to consider these challenges, researchers have often retreated to investigating binary (false/true) examples of misinformation, here nobody would argue against the examples of misinformation––solving the ethical problem. 
Third, conceptual choices are often shaped by methodological constraints (e.g., the type of data that is available) or disciplinary preferences (e.g., the methodological traditions), which is why misinformation is often used as a general umbrella term for different phenomena \citep{weeksWhatNextSix2021}.
Consequently, this semantic variance has contributed to imprecise or inconclusive findings regarding the prevalence and impact of misinformation. 
While some scholars, such as \citet{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a}, argue that the effects of misinformation are overstated, calling it a symptom of broader social issues, others, like \cite{tayThinkingClearlyMisinformation2024}, reason that the heterogeneity in measuring misinformation contributes to divergent evidence and creates a false dichotomy between understanding misinformation as a symptom or a cause.

To study the diffusion or discussion of misinformation on social media, computational researchers commonly opt for either story- or source-based methodologies. Both approaches rely on experts, such as fact-checkers, to curate lists of stories or sources, which then serve as the foundation for collecting data from digital platforms. These approaches, however, operate at different levels of analysis, and the choice between them depends on the primary research objective. When the goal is to study specific instances of misinformation with high certainty, e.g., focusing on a specific case, the story-based approach proves advantageous. By selecting a handful of (fact-checked) stories, researchers can ensure low variance in accuracy ratings and achieve high precision in capturing misinformation. For instance, Vosoughi et al. \citeyear{vosoughiSpreadTrueFalse2018a} traced the diffusion of rumors on Twitter (now X) using links to fact-checked stories, while Charquero-Ballester et al. \citeyear{charquero-ballesterDifferentTypesCOVID192021} tried to scale up URL-searches by classifying tweets mentioning keywords related to fact-checked false stories. Additionally, this approach frees researchers of the responsibility to judge the trustworthiness of individual stories, as experts have already verified factual components of the stories, and identified components of the story that are false.

However, relying solely on a small selection of fact-checked stories has its limitations. Singular stories, while clearly false and widely debated, represent only a fraction of misinformation present on social media platforms, and it is impossible to generalize findings beyond these specific, potentially biased cases. 
Moreover, these forms of operationalization may not encapsulate the full spectrum of misinformation and largely neglect more subtle (likely not fact-checked) and harder-to-detect forms of misinformation that have been shown to be more persistent \citep{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a, hameleersDegreesDeceptionEffects2021a}. 
Furthermore, the selection bias inherent in fact-checking organizations' coverage may skew the representation of misinformation, neglecting topics for which they lack expertise at a given time. 
Using fact-checked stories also reflects one of the limitations of corrective intervention strategies: it is a rather slow, reactive process that may be ineffective for real-time actions.
Consequently, this approach tends to underestimate both the prevalence and impact of misinformation, failing to capture and judge the harmfulness of its broader dynamics effectively. 

Source-based strategies offer the advantage of encompassing a broader spectrum of sources and narratives, thus enriching the sample by considering a more diverse information environment. Source-based approaches also enable the inclusion of subtler forms of misinformation and facilitate accurate estimations of both the scale \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020, grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019,guessExposureUntrustworthyWebsites2020a, yangCOVID19InfodemicTwitter2021a} and dynamics of dissemination \citep{Lasser2022, pennycookShiftingAttentionAccuracy2021a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a, shaoSpreadLowcredibilityContent2018}. Nevertheless, it's important to note that not every story originating from an untrustworthy source is necessarily false, introducing a degree of inaccuracy at the level of individual articles. Despite potential accuracy errors at this granularity, source-based judgments of trustworthiness can be accurate at the macroscopic level. For example, the same source may publish accurate reports of events, but they may also publish inaccurate opinion pieces. Hence, variance in article accuracy can be covered by a lower trustworthiness score at the level of the source. Another example is that unreliable publishers who predominantly share unreliable news articles may occasionally publish reliable news pieces to attract new (or keep existing) audiences.

The precision of source-based approaches hinges largely on the selection and the quality of ratings of sources within a given list. Strictly speaking, this conceptualization covers information disseminated by unreliable sources -- making it essential to recognize that observed patterns may be intricately linked to the inherent biases or external forces in the selection of sources. For instance, an unbalanced selection of unreliable sources may reveal patterns due to that imbalance rather than patterns unique to misinformation. This underscores the importance of also assessing factors such as political biases or editorial practices of the sources, considering that a lack of editorial oversight can compromise the quality of news reporting \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}. In other words, to obtain a balanced selection of sources and account for the influence of source-related variables other than reliability, source-based approaches can incorporate assessments of political, cultural, or editorial biases and misleading tendencies while maintaining generalizability. Nonetheless, a number of problems remain in practice: First, source categorizations often oversimplify the nuanced spectrum of misinformation, often relying on a binary classification system (fake/real). Second, it is crucial to acknowledge that source ratings vary across time and cultural contexts, for example, following a change in ownership or editorial practices. Third, while source judgments are typically made without requiring specific topic expertise, they often reflect the level of journalistic professionalism and editorial norms of a particular media ecosystem, which may not be universally applicable across different contexts \citep{bruggemannHallinManciniRevisited2014b}.

Taken together, source-based approaches are a promising way to track misinformation online. However, to study the spread of unreliable news, scientists need access to reliable and credible databases of source ratings. Since the 2016 US elections, the most popular way to track misinformation online has been to use a list of untrustworthy or unreliable sources and search for their web domains in URL text \citep{Lasser2022, grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019, guessExposureUntrustworthyWebsites2020a, shaoSpreadLowcredibilityContent2018, yangCOVID19InfodemicTwitter2021a}. The organization \href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/}{NewsGuard} offers the most comprehensive list of such domains \cite{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}. It does not just blacklist untrustworthy sources but provides a continuous trustworthiness score for each source, covering news sources across the entire spectrum of news quality. A trained team of experts, consisting mostly of journalists and editors, rates the news sources based on nine journalistic quality criteria and regularly updates both the coverage of the list (increasing the number of sources) and their overall trustworthiness rating. The database is constantly growing and has been extended to countries beyond the United States in the last years, for example, Germany and Italy. With recent political events and crises, demand for such databases has increased, enhancing the popularity of NewsGuard. For instance, it has been used to study user engagement with unreliable news during the 2016 and 2020 US elections \citet{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a} or to assess the changing quality of cited information of political elites in the US \citep{Lasser2022}. 

However, the NewsGuard database was initially created as a tool for brand security rather than scientific research. In addition, employing journalists and editors to assess the sources included in the list incurs significant expenses. Given the predominantly private audience and expenses, the NewsGuard database is only available to subscribers who pay a significant fee. Therefore, researchers in need of a method to quantify misinformation need to carefully weigh the mentioned disadvantages of source-based approaches and the investment necessary to acquire access to the NewsGuard database. To aid this decision, here we provide a comprehensive assessment of the content and reliability of this database. We aim to answer the following main research question: How reliable is the NewsGuard database of source trustworthiness ratings over time and language contexts? For this, we first assess the distribution of trustworthiness ratings, the process of updating them, and the selection and removal of sources over time. Second, we assess the completeness of the database for different countries. Lastly, we estimate the usefulness of other information provided in the database, such as the ratings for individual journalistic criteria and the political orientation of sources. In order to assess different aspects of the database, we introduce three sub-questions:

\textbf{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings, e.g., how often do ratings change or are sources removed?} 
Investigating the development of the NewsGuard database over time is important to estimate whether using an older version of the database will distort results and whether an older version of the database should be used to measure trustworthiness in older data. We describe the different versions of the database and provide an exemplary reproduction of published research relying on the NewsGuard list with different versions of the list to assess the degree to which results depend on a specific version of the list. 

\textbf{How stable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?} A recent study has shown high agreement of NewsGuard with other expert-rated lists in the US context~\citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}, but an assessment for other countries is missing. Therefore, we next compare the distribution and completeness of information provided by NewsGuard across different contexts. 

\textbf{How can other source labels in the NewsGuard database be used for misinformation research?} NewsGuard experts categorize each source according to nine binary journalistic quality criteria. The criteria are weighted and aggregated to a quasi-continuous trustworthiness score. In addition, NewsGuard labels the topics covered as well as the political orientation of the source. %Analyzing the importance of journalistic criteria to trustworthiness ratings as well as the completeness of additional information might shed light on aspects of misinformation beyond binary true/false labels used in other lists.

By assessing the database, we outline potential pitfalls for digital media research and offer recommendations for the use of the NewsGuard database.

\section{Dataset construction}
NewsGuard collects the list of sources it rates by tracking online activity. On their website, NewsGuard claims to have reviewed news sources accounting for 95\% of online engagement. The organization also manually adds sources they deem influential, or that changed their web domain. The database automatically updates every hour and allows tracking changes back to March 2019, when the organization started operating. Still, the procedure by which domains are selected or removed is undisclosed on the website, as are the intervals at which the trustworthiness assessments are updated. 

As of June 15, 2024, the database has 11,998 entries in total, some without a trustworthiness rating. This includes 70 sources classified as a ``platform'' (e.g., YouTube), 63 sources judged as ``satire'' (e.g., The Onion) and 731 lifestyle sources (e.g., healthquotefree.com). NewsGuard does not rate these sources, but they make up 7.2\% of the listed sources in the database. For the remaining 11,134 sources, the database includes the following characteristics (see Table~\ref{table:tablea1} for a full list of variables):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unique identifier for each rating and date of the last update
    \item Domain name and parent domain (e.g., nytimes.com)
    \item A trustworthiness rating as a quasi-continuous scale (between 0 and 100) and binary labels (N = Not Trustworthy/T = Trustworthy) based on a threshold at 60
    \item Language and country associated with the source
    \item Political orientation of the source (e.g., ``left'' and ``right'')
    \item Topics covered (e.g., ``local news'', ``political news'', ``health information'') by the source
\end{itemize}

The team of NewsGuard experts regularly judges the trustworthiness of the selected news sources based on nine journalistic criteria \footnote{\href{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}} that are treated the same for different types of sources and from different countries. The trustworthiness score is a composite score of these nine indicators. More precisely, experts assign a binary label for each criterion, each awarding a number of points that together constitute the overall trustworthiness rating. The criteria are weighted differently, ordered in descending relevance:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'', changed to ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' in December 2023 (22 points)
  \item ``Gathers and presents information responsibly'' (18 points) 
  \item ``Regularly corrects or clarifies errors'', changed to ``Has effective practices for correcting errors'' in December 2023 (12.5 points)
  \item ``Handles the difference between news and opinion responsibly'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' (10 points)
  \item ``Website discloses ownership and financing' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Clearly labels advertising'' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Reveals who's in charge, including possible conflicts of interest'' (5 points)
  \item ``The site provides the names of content creators, along with either contact or biographical information'' (5 points)
\end{enumerate}

Since the overall score is a sum of the individual scores for each criterion, it is not truly continuous, as some values (for example, 1, 2, 3, or 4 points) cannot be achieved by any combination of criteria.

\section{Descriptive Analyses}
\subsection{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings?}
In the following, we review how the database, and particularly the trustworthiness ratings and source selection, have changed over the years since the first version of the database was compiled in 2019.

\textbf{Coverage across time.} Over the span of five years, NewsGuard has grown from 2,615 to 11,134 sources with ratings, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}. NewsGuard regularly adds new sources but rarely removes any, i.e., the database has been growing to three times its initial size. When counting the added domains based on monthly comparisons of the list, NewsGuard has added 8,616 domains over time in total but only removed 669. More precisely, the NewsGuard team adds an average of 139 new domains per month.

NewsGuard ratings are available for customers in an online database (Amazon S3 bucket), where a current snapshot is uploaded every hour. We sampled a snapshot from the first hour of the 15th of every month. When we sampled from the second day of each month as a test, we observed two irregular dips in the number of sources (see May 2022 and February 2024 in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}A). It is likely that errors caused this fluctuation. For instance, in May 2022, the approximately 3,500 removed sources were added again two weeks later. Yet, this sudden disappearance and reappearance of sources illustrate the overall volatility of the database. We therefore advise researchers to inspect several database snapshots and check their consistency before choosing one. 

The most recent snapshot contains ratings for only 10,588 unique domains (compared to 11,998 entries in total); in other words, some sources appear multiple times. Typically, these sources publish in different languages, i.e., they have the same web domain but provide multi-lingual content, or they have an entry as a local and a global source. For instance, the Austrian website servustv.com produces both German- and English-language output and appears with the country label ``AT'' and the global label ``ALL''. Except for three cases, duplicated domains have identical trustworthiness ratings. Therefore, dropping duplicated domain entries does not substantially affect downstream research results. However, if a classification of sources by country is necessary for the research question at hand, researchers should keep duplicated domains. 

Furthermore, 5,574 of the rated domains belong to only 433 unique parent domains. 
Some parent domains have multiple variations of the domain name, probably representing the same source, e.g., theragingpatriot.org has domains ending with .blog, .com, .net,  and .pro. Other parent domains have very different sub-domains, e.g., 24usnews.com and africdaily.com belong to charmdaily.com. 
The other 6,424 ratings do not have a parent domain entry in the database. 
Domains belonging to the same parent domain usually have the same trustworthiness rating.
Furthermore, NewsGuard updates domains belonging to the same parent domain at once, and they seem to assign the same unique identifier to those domains. 

\textbf{Distribution.} NewsGuard trustworthiness ratings range from 0 to 100. Historically, NewsGuard considered sources with a rating below 60 as not trustworthy, therefore assigning binary trustworthiness labels\footnote{Snapshot of the described rating process from December 31st, 2022 \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. Since February 2023\footnote{Snapshot of the updated rating process from February 1st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}, NewsGuard uses a more granular categorization, classifying sources with a score of 100 as "high credibility", scores between 75 and 99 as "generally credible", scores between 60 and 74 as "credible with exceptions", scores between 40 and 59 as "proceed with caution", and scores between 0 and 39 as "proceed with maximum caution". Notably, NewsGuard is not a blacklist. In fact, close to 60\% of sources have an overall rating of over 60 (see Fig.~\ref{fig:fig1}B for the distribution of trustworthiness scores). 

Furthermore, the distributions also clearly show a multimodal structure, indicative of the quasi-continuous nature of the overall NewsGuard score. In addition, some combinations of characteristics seem to be much more prevalent than others, indicating that the individual characteristics are not independent of each other (see also ``Composition of trustworthiness score'' below).

When looking at the score distribution over time, the skew towards high ratings diminishes slightly: while the average score was 71.8 (SD=33.3) in 2019, it is now at 62.9 (SD=33.1). The distributions of trustworthiness from 2019 to 2024 are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}C. Various explanations for decreases in trustworthiness over time are possible. First, the database has tripled in size and added many sources with lower trustworthiness scores (on average 60.2, SD=27.5). Especially during the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, a great number of sources with lower ratings were added. The highest number of sources added was in September 2020, when 1,030 sources with, on average, very low trustworthiness (M=35.3, SD=14.1) were included. It is unclear if this development is due to an effort by NewsGuard to include more untrustworthy news or a global increase in untrustworthy sources. 

Second, NewsGuard employs teams of experts per country, potentially leading to differences in coverage and ratings in different countries. Dips in trustworthiness may be related to country-specific sources being added (e.g., 1,015 of the 1,030 added sources in September 2020 are coded as US; see Figure~\ref{figa1} for the number of sources added per country over time). In contrast, adding entirely new countries seems to have little effect on the trustworthiness scores (see Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}). Overall, smaller dips appear across all countries covered and are most likely related to many low-quality sources gaining traction around the time of the COVID-19 pandemic.

A third possibility is updates of existing ratings. NewsGuard indicates the date when a rating was last updated, even if the score did not change. Updates to entries happen on average once every eight months (243 days), but this varies strongly per source between a few seconds to a maximum of 1,109 days (interquartile range: 162 to 355 days). When updated, trustworthiness scores mostly remain unchanged, i.e., the majority of sources have not decreased in trustworthiness. However, a few sources drastically dropped in trustworthiness; for instance, conservativedailynews.com lost 80 points in one update in November 2020. Figure~\ref{fig:figa2} illustrates the differences in consecutive scores over time, with major score updates in early 2020. Overall, the decrease in trustworthiness that we can see in the right-hand plot in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2} can, therefore, be attributed to NewsGuard adding untrustworthy sources rather than previously trustworthy sources losing their reliability. This interpretation is also supported by our analysis of changes in individual rating criteria below.

\textbf{Composition of trustworthiness score.} 
The trustworthiness score is a composite score that is calculated based on nine journalistic criteria. For each source, NewsGuard's raters assign a Boolean value (yes/no) per criterion. The trustworthiness score is then calculated as the sum of the weighted criteria. Because of the discrete point contributions to the overall score, the resulting trustworthiness score has a limited number of values it can take based on the different combinations of categories. Further explanation of the criteria and their weights can be found in the NewsGuard FAQ\footnote{Snapshot taken from December 31st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. 

In the latest version of the dataset, 83.6\% of sources fulfill the criterion ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' (8,903 sources). The second most prevalent criterion is ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'', which is met by 82\% of all sources. These two top criteria often go together: When one of them is satisfied, the other one tends to be as well (Pearson's correlation coefficient = 0.88). 
A factor analysis with oblique rotation confirms that the two criteria load on one factor, accounting for 56.4\% of the explained variance, with factor loadings > 0.9. All other criteria share 36.5\% of the variance explained (factor loadings >.6). 
%Meeting any of the two criteria strongly predicts a high rating (with regression coefficients of \(\beta\)=61.91, p<.001, and \(\beta\)=61.94, p<.001, respectively). 
In line with that, 13.5\% of all sources meet both of the top criteria combined, while 10.2\% of sources satisfy none of the nine journalistic criteria. The most common combination of criteria is that all nine criteria are satisfied at the same time (15.7\% of ratings). We provide a correlation matrix of criteria in the Appendix (Table~\ref{table:tablea1}.

We find that most sources do not change which journalistic criteria they fulfill during updates. The most common change is sources that stop disclosing ownership and financing, with 12\% of score updates being related to a change in this criterion. On the other hand, a positive change for this criterion is the third most common pattern, occurring in 8.6\% of score updates. This indicates that NewsGuard might frequently check criteria related to ownership. We present update patterns of individual criteria over time in Figure~\ref{fig:fig4}.

\textbf{Reproducibility of downstream research results using different snapshots of the database.}
Over the observation period, we observe both the addition of a substantial amount of sources, as well as some changes in the fulfillment of individual journalistic criteria and therefore, the overall score for individual sources. This begets the question of whether such changes can potentially influence the conclusions drawn from investigations that rely on the NewsGuard database, depending on which version of the database is used.
To provide a partial answer to this question, we reproduce some of the analyses of the trustworthiness ratings of sources shared by politicians in the US, UK and Germany by Lasser et al. \cite{Lasser2022}, using different versions of the NewsGuard database.
In Figure~\ref{fig:reproduction} A, we show the temporal development of the average NewsGuard score of sources shared by Democrat and Republican members of Congress on Twitter between 2016 and 2022.
We use snapshots of the NewsGuard database taken on March 1 of each year between 2019 and 2024 (the original research used the snapshot from March 1, 2022).
For Republicans, the result barely changes between different versions of the database, with the exception of the snapshot from 2019, which results in substantially higher scores—on average 3.2 points higher than when using the snapshot from 2020.
The differences between snapshots from other years do not surpass 0.3 points and have no trend. 
For Democrats, the snapshot from 2019 also has substantially higher scores (2.1 points when compared to 2020).
In addition, the snapshot from 2024 yields a substantially lower average score when compared to the snapshot from 2023 with a difference of 1.3 points. For other years, the differences also do not surpass 0.3 points and have no trend.
However, even given the sometimes substantial differences in scores, the main finding of the research, namely the increasing difference in average scores between Democrats and Republicans, does not change.
Figure~\ref{fig:reproduction} B shows the average difference in the NewsGuard score of sources shared by the politicians over time. For 2019, the snapshot that deviates most strongly from the other snapshots, the difference is smaller in the years 2020 to 2022 but remains substantial.

The finding that for the US, there are only minor changes depending on which version of the database is used is likely largely due to the fact that sources rarely change their rating, and that for the US, almost all major news sources were already covered in the first version of the NewsGuard database: the snapshot from 2019 covers 15.0\% of links posted by Democrats on Twitter and 18.5\% of links posted by Republicans. The links not covered by NewsGuard are mostly links to platforms, campaign sites etc., see also supplement of \cite{Lasser2022} for a detailed analysis of links not covered by NewsGuard. For the snapshot from 2024, the coverage slightly increases to 16.3\% and 19.6\% of links for Democrats and Republicans, respectively.

For the other two countries, Germany and the UK, the picture changes: differences in average ratings stratified by party show larger differences between years (see Figures~\ref{fig:reproduction_DE} and~\ref{fig:reproduction_UK} in the appendix). This is likely due to massive changes in the coverage of the sources shared by politicians in these countries by NewsGuard. For example, for members of the German party SPD, coverage increased from 0.5\% in 2019 to 12.7\% in 2024 and from 0.2\% to 12.0\% for members of CDU/CSU. For smaller parties like DIE LINKE and the Greens, using different versions of the database also leads to different conclusions: for the 2019 version, it seems like there is an upward trend in the average NewsGuard score of linked sources, whereas for other versions, this trend is absent. 
In the UK, the coverage for members of the Labour Party increased from 7.8\% to 13.5\%, and for Tories, it increased from 2.4\% to 7.8\%. In the UK, the main conclusion of the reproduced research, namely that there is no trend in the average NewsGuard score, would not have changed depending on the database version used. This analysis shows that for countries other than the US, the usage of different versions of the database has the potential to significantly influence research outcomes, likely due to the addition of a significant number of relevant new sources to the database over the years. Researchers investigating countries for which sources were only recently added to the database should, therefore, proceed with caution and invest time into validating the coverage of the database for the given country.

\textbf{Comparison with other lists.} In order to measure the actual coverage of sources per country, it is necessary to compare the sources covered by NewsGuard with other lists. However, other lists are often not compiled with the same objective as NewsGuard, which is to cover all sources responsible for most of the internet traffic. Therefore, their scope and rating system may differ. In addition, other lists are not available for every country. 

For the US context, ~\citet{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023} have compared five lists of existing expert ratings with NewsGuard and found a high number of non-overlapping web domains between lists. However, ratings correlated significantly for the web domains included in multiple lists. Further, Puschmann et al. (2024) collected a list of German online news domains (GOND)\footnote{\href{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}}, largely relying on web tracking data of a representative sample of 1,500 German citizens. In the first version of the list, the authors also classified the domains into types, among others ``legacy press'' (49\%), ``digital-born news outlet'' (18.0\%), or ``hyperpartisan news'' (8.2\%). GOND has 543 global sources in common with NewsGuard, including 147 English-language, 47 French, and 56 Italian sources. Out of all global sources that overlap, 285 are legacy press, and 56 are hyperpartisan news. Furthermore, GOND covers the same 237 German sources as NewsGuard, including 24 hyperpartisan and 19 digital-born news outlets. Overall, it seems that NewsGuard's coverage of the United States and Germany is extensive, while comparisons for other countries are missing.  
 
\subsection{How can other information be used for misinformation research?}
\textbf{Political Orientation.} The dataset also classifies sources according to their political orientation, relying on a left-to-right conception of the political spectrum. In December 2022, the categories were condensed from four (far left/ slightly left/slightly right/far right) to two (left/right). Overall, the political orientation label is only available for a minority of the sources. In the current version of the database, 31.2\% of the sources have a label for political orientation (2,980 sources). For countries other than the US, where 41.1\% of news outlets have a political orientation label, only a small minority of sources have such a label: 9.8\% of sources associated with Great Britain, 11.6\% for Italy, 16.1\% for Germany, and 15.3\% for France. Here, again, the over-representation of the United States in the data set becomes apparent. Table~\ref{table:table4} shows the total number of political orientation labels per country as well as the number and percentage of sources classified as left or right-leaning in the most recent version of the database.
In most countries, the majority of sources with political orientation labels belong to the right-wing spectrum. Figure~\ref{fig:figa5} shows the distribution of political orientation per country over time.

Trustworthiness ratings are generally lower for right-leaning sources. In the current database (December 2023), the mean trustworthiness score for right-leaning sources is 26.7 (SD=23.4), in contrast to 61.7 (SD=20.1) for left-leaning sources (Mdiff=35.0, t(2978)=32.4, p < .001). In August/September 2021, however, when a high number of untrustworthy sources were added to the database, particularly the trustworthiness of slightly right-leaning sources dropped by almost 23 points, see Figure~\ref{fig:fig6}. When the label became binary, this led to a lower average score of right-leaning sources by adding low-quality sources classified as "far-right" before (where trustworthiness ranged between 13.5 and 17.8 points).

\textbf{Topics.} NewsGuard experts assign topic labels based on the topics covered by the sources, which can also be found in the database. A source is usually assigned to multiple topics. The topic ratings were introduced around October 2020 and steadily increased over time. In the most recent version of the database, only 161 sources are without a topic label (1.7\%). The most popular topics are ``Local News'' (39.1\% of sources), ``Political News or Commentary'' (37.9\%), ``General News'' (19.9\%), ``Health or Medical Information'' (10.5\%), and ``Conspiracy Theories or Hoaxes'' (9.5\%).

\section{Conclusion}
An inherent bias in misinformation research lies in the definitions of false information and its measurement methodologies. This review first describes source- and story-based approaches for tracking online misinformation and assesses their reliability, validity, and feasibility. Second, we describe the NewsGuard database which contains trustworthiness ratings of over 10,000 sources based on journalistic criteria and requires a license to use for research. To date, NewsGuard is the most comprehensive database of source-level trustworthiness scores, selecting the sources it rates based on web traffic data and employing professional editors and journalists to compile and update its ratings. We discuss the content of the data base and its usefulness for misinformation research relating to its completeness, volatility and additional information contained in the data base next to trustworthiness ratings. We discuss the implications of our findings and provide recommendations for utilizing the NewsGuard database for research.

The database contains ratings for sources from nine countries. It extensively covers sources based in the United States, with substantially lower coverage for the other eight countries. Still, source ratings are regularly updated across all countries with at most on average 399 days between updates in Germany and as little as 235 days in New Zealand. Over 50\% of the sources included in the database have a score of 60 points or higher and are therefore considered at least ``credible with exceptions'' according to NewsGuard's nomenclature. NewsGuard contains more right- than left-leaning sources, with the former generally rated as less trustworthy. However, as the ratings provided by NewsGuard are largely consistent with ratings provided by other independent fact checking organisations~\cite{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}, it is unlikely that this difference is caused by an inherent bias against right-leaning sources in NewsGuard's rating processes. 

Examining the volatility of trustworthiness ratings, we find that changes in source coverage can be abrupt, with several instances of over 1000 sources being removed from or added to the data base in a single update. %requiring that researchers carefully assess several database snapshots for consistency before choosing which one to use. 
In general, NewsGuard provides a new snapshot of its database every hour but does not disclose its process for updating the information contained in it. Analysing the time stamps of entries for sources from different countries we find that information in the database is likely regularly checked on a per-country basis, but that changes to trustworthiness ratings of sources happen only infrequently and typically following changes in website ownership or editorial practices (e.g., related to how sources label advertising or handle errors). Even then, changes in trustworthiness ratings are usually trivial. 

Different sources have a varying degree of popularity in terms of how often they are linked to or cited, and will therefore have different importance for research. To account for this fact and shed some light on the impact of changes in the NewsGuard database on downstream research outcomes, we reproduced research investigating news-sharing practices or political elites in the US, Germany, and the UK~\cite{Lasser2022}. We find that after 2019, the content of the database largely reached a stable state in the US, and using different versions of the database between 2020 and 2024 does not change research outcomes. For Germany and the UK, this process took longer but after 2022 we also only observed minor changes in these countries. Despite these nuances, the trustworthiness ratings by NewsGuard appear relatively insensitive to time, speaking for the reliability of source-based approaches. Given our observations, we suggest that researchers using NewsGuard examine several snapshots of the database to ensure no major source additions or deletions occurred recently. For countries that have been included in NewsGuard for longer, e.g., the US, Canada, France, Italy, Germany and the UK, we suggest that researchers use whatever version of the database (after 2022) they have access to. For more recent additions, e.g., Australia, Austria and New Zealand, we suggest that researchers always use the most recent version of the database and carefully examine it for differences in coverage for different units of analysis, such as political leaning.  

Furthermore, we compared ratings across countries and considered alternative lists to evaluate the validity of the NewsGuard database, particularly outside the US context. We found that country-specific factors significantly influence coverage, with disparities evident among countries sharing similar media systems. While the coverage for English-speaking countries (US and U.K.) is extensive, coverage for countries such as Italy, France, and Germany varies. Even though the countries covered have similar media systems, the journalistic criteria may not be applicable to all, as the distinct patterns in Italy and France in contrast to the other countries suggest. According to Brüggemann et al. \citeyear{bruggemannHallinManciniRevisited2014b} analysis of media systems, Italy and France belong to the Southern cluster that has a less professionalized journalism sector than the Central (e.g., Germany) or Western clusters (e.g., US). According to Hallin \& Mancini (2004), in their seminal definition of media systems, those in countries that fail to meet the professionalism indicators lack an understanding of serving the public interest. In other words, when journalistic professionalism is low, journalists lack professional autonomy and often serve economic or political interests. This may explain the lack of transparency in these countries, as indicated by NewsGuards' journalistic criteria. The criteria that NewsGuard uses to judge the trustworthiness of sources thus seem to broadly reflect differences in media systems––at least for the Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic countries covered. Nonetheless, the discrepancy between Southern and other countries underscores that applications of the database need to be tailored to individual contexts.

Next to the trustworthiness ratings, the NewsGuard data base includes other information such as the political leaning of sources, and the topics they cover. This information could potentially be useful for misinformation research, but to date there is no systematic assessment of its completeness and potential biases. Overall, only a minority of sources have a label indicating its political leaning. While for the US, the fraction is relatively high (41\%), in other countries only roughly one in ten sources have such a label. In addition, the majority of sources that have a label are right-leaning. 
This raises the question of whether this disparity results from a sampling bias of NewsGuard editors rather than a bias on the supply side. Research on media biases suggests a prevalence of left-leaning opinions in major mainstream sources \citep{osmundsenPartisanPolarizationPrimary2021}, leading to the emergence of smaller fringe outlets on the right. This would also explain lower trustworthiness on the right-wing spectrum, which is in line with the political asymmetry repeatedly observed in susceptibility to misinformation, e.g., recently by Robertson et al. \citeyear{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. NewsGuard also discusses this topic in their 2023 Social Impact Report\footnote{https://web.archive.org/web/20240228003711/https://www.newsguardtech.com/special-reports/social-impact-report-2023/}, emphasizing that their trustworthiness ratings are intended to be apolitical. Yet, when sampling based on the NewsGuard list and interpreting the prevalence of misinformation across political spectra, this political bias may reproduce in downstream research results. Overall, political orientation ratings, especially outside the US, should be interpreted with caution due to potential incompleteness and sampling biases. 

Lastly, news topic and journalistic criteria ratings enrich source characterization and offer valuable insights into misinformation dynamics. %anything else to say about the topics?
