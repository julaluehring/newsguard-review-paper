\begin{abstract}
\noindent To study the spread of misinformation on digital platforms, it is imperative for computational social scientists to have access to reliable and valid tools that enable them to identify misinformation. A prominent way to track misinformation is to use a list of domains. The organization NewsGuard offers the most comprehensive database of domain trustworthiness ratings and is increasing in popularity. Despite restrictive access, the database was neither created as a research instrument nor has it ever been reviewed systematically for this purpose. Therefore, we offer a comprehensive description and inspection of the NewsGuard database, across the following dimensions: the reliability of trustworthiness ratings, the selection of domains, as well as the completeness of categories such as political orientation. By describing the database over time and across countries, we outline potential pitfalls and recommendations for digital media research and discuss appropriate use cases. 
\end{abstract}
\keywords{\textit{misinformation, NewsGuard, social media} \vspace{8ex}}

\section{Introduction}
Misinformation permeates both social media platforms \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018} and mainstream media channels \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. However, only a fraction of news consumption is attributed to false news — roughly ~0.15\% \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020} — and only a small group of users actively engage with unreliable information online \citep{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. Typically, these individuals hold strong political or ideological beliefs, accepting information that echoes their opinions and often coming from established sources. Consequently, it is unsurprising that misinformation exacerbates partisan divides and fuels inter-group hostility \citep{gonzalez-bailonAsymmetricIdeologicalSegregation2023a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}, capitalizing on these dynamics to stoke conflict and evoke negative emotions. With decreasing levels of trust in the media (Newman et al., 2020) and institutions (Bennett \& Livingston, 2020), it has become more critical to understand such dynamics reinforcing the dissemination of misinformation and their relationship with digital media. 

The spread of misinformation has been studied extensively by scholars from various scientific disciplines, relying on diverse conceptualizations. In psychology, researchers commonly define misinformation as inaccurate or unreliable information \citep{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022}, while in communication science, a popular definition is that by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan (\citeyear{wardleInformationDisorderInterdisciplinary2017}), distinguishing misinformation from disinformation and fake news that pursues financial or political intentions. Computational social scientists, for instance, David Lazer and colleagues (\citeyear{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}), follow this notion:
"Fake news overlaps with other information disorders, such as misinformation (false or misleading information) and disinformation (false information that is purposely spread to deceive people)." (p. 1094)

Inconsistent definitions leave researchers with a number of degrees of freedom. Here, researchers themselves can define the boundaries of misinformation, or in other words, they can decide where to draw the line on truth. In reality, such conceptual choices largely depend on methodological constraints or preferences by journals or conferences, which is why the terms are often used interchangeably. This has led to imprecise or inconclusive findings about the prevalence of misinformation and its effects. While \citet{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a} and many others make the observation that the effects of misinformation are overstated and advertise it as a symptom of social causes, \cite{tayThinkingClearlyMisinformation2024} argues that heterogeneity in how we measure misinformation explains this heterogeneity in evidence and creates a false dichotomy of understanding misinformation as a symptom or a cause.

To study the diffusion or discussion of misinformation on social media, computational researchers commonly follow a selection of stories or sources, thereby investigating the phenomenon at different levels of analysis. Both approaches typically rely on experts, such as fact-checkers, to curate a list that is then used as a starting point to collect data from one or more digital platforms. The choice between story- and source-based approaches hinges on the primary research objective: When operating at the level of the story, we find low variance in accuracy ratings, and we can capture misinformation with high precision, i.e., we can be sure that what we measure is related to false information. However, the selection of stories can be narrow, and it is nearly impossible to generalize findings beyond a specific, potentially biased case. \citet{vosoughiSpreadTrueFalse2018a}, for example, selected fact-checked fake news stories and followed their diffusion on Twitter. This approach has the advantage that researchers can rely on experts to determine what is objectively false; the researcher does not have to judge the trustworthiness of the stories themselves. On the other hand, misinformation taken from fact-checking websites is common and often widely debated. A lot of times, fact-checking organizations cover topics that they have experts for at that time. Therefore, the sample only represents a small proportion of misinformation and neglects more subtle types of misinformation that are harder to identify. While the story-level approach captures dynamics related to some types of misinformation, results tend to underestimate both the reach of misinformation and the effects thereof. %%add more example refs

Conversely, source-based strategies can cover a wider variety of sources and stories, and hence, samples scale up to a more diverse information environment. By sampling based on sources, we can include more subtle types of misinformation and get accurate estimates
both for the scale \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020, grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019,guessExposureUntrustworthyWebsites2020a} and dynamics of its spread \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a, pennycookShiftingAttentionAccuracy2021a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. But even in a case where we overgeneralize across articles and the error rate for the article accuracy is high, source judgments can still be unbiased at the macro level. Unreliable publishers publish mostly unreliable news pieces, but they may intentionally post reliable news pieces once in a while to attract new readers. The precision of such approaches depends on the selection of sources, but research has shown that lists for U.S.-based sources largely overlap \citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}. Now, if we are conceptually rigorous, misinformation in this context is defined by unreliable sources, and discernible patterns may be more closely associated with the source itself, including its political bias or editorial practices, rather than solely with misinformation. Indeed, a lack of editorial practices may lead to lower quality in news stories \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}. In order to exclude other source-related factors, this approach can take source biases and misleadingness into consideration while also being generalizable. In reality, however, categorizations of sources are made on a binary scale, neglecting more subtle forms of misinformation. Furthermore, this argument ignores that source ratings are sensitive to time and context. Sources may change, for instance, due to ownership. In addition, while source judgments can be made irrespective of topic expertise, such ratings are often based on journalistic norms of one media system that are not necessarily applicable to other contexts.

In conclusion, we see source-based approaches as a promising way to track misinformation online. However, for computational social scientists to study the spread of unreliable news, they require access to reliable and credible databases of source ratings. Since the 2016 U.S. elections, the most prominent way to track misinformation online has been to use a list of untrustworthy or unreliable domains \citep[e.g.,][]{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. The organization \href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/}{NewsGuard} offers the most comprehensive database, not just blacklisting domains but providing a trustworthiness score per domain. A team of journalists and editors rate the domains based on nine journalistic quality criteria and regularly updates both the coverage of the list, i.e., the number of domains, as well as their overall trustworthiness rating. Recently, the database has been extended to other countries than the United States and is constantly growing. With recent political events and crises, there has been a heightened demand for such databases, leading to an increase in NewsGuard's popularity. For instance, it has been used to study user's engagement with unreliable news in the context of the 2018 and 2020 US elections \citet{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a} or to assess if members of the Republican party in the U.S. Congress have contributed to the spread of misinformation by linking to untrustworthy sources \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a}. However, the database was created as a tool for brand security rather than research, which is why access is paywalled and expensive. In light of the discussed disadvantages of list-based approaches, we here provide a comprehensive description of the content of this database following the research question: How reliable is the NewsGuard database of domain trustworthiness ratings over time and language contexts? 

More precisely, we believe that it is essential to address the following aspects of the NewsGuard database in more detail: First, we look into the distribution and update process of the trustworthiness ratings as well as the selection and removal of sources over time. Second, we assess the reliability of the database for different countries, and lastly, we estimate the usefulness of other information provided in the database, e.g., journalistic criteria and political orientation. Therefore, we focus on three questions:

\textbf{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings, e.g., how often do ratings change or do domains vanish?} 
First, we investigate the development of the NewsGuard database over time. This is important to estimate whether using an older database will distort results. We describe the different versions of the database and reproduce published research relying on the NewsGuard list with different versions of the list to assess the degree to which results depend on a specific version of the list. 

\textbf{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?} A recent study has shown high agreement of NewsGuard with other expert-rated lists in the U.S. context~\citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}, but an assessment for other countries and contexts is missing. Therefore, we next compare the distribution and completeness of information provided by NewsGuard across different contexts. 

\textbf{How can other information be used for misinformation research?} For each source, NewsGuard experts rate journalistic quality criteria, topics, and political orientation, but they also give sources flags for issues. Estimating to what extent the different criteria constitute the overall trustworthiness score and the completeness of additional information might shed light on aspects of misinformation beyond binary true/false labels. 

By describing the database, we outline potential pitfalls for digital media research and offer recommendations for the application of the NewsGuard database in different contexts. %short summary of findings
We also want to empower misinformation researchers to make informed decisions about whether the substantial expense associated with acquiring access to NewsGuard is necessary for their research. 

\section{Dataset construction}
NewsGuard collects the list of sources to rate from tracking online activity. On their website, NewsGuard claims to have reviewed domains accounting for 95\% of online engagement. The organization also manually adds sources they deem influential or that changed domains. The database automatically updates every hour and allows us to track changes going back to March 2019, when the organization started operating. Still, the procedure by which domains are selected or removed is undisclosed on the website, as are the intervals in which the trustworthiness assessments are updated in the database. 

As of November 15, 2023, the database covers 11,093 unique sources. Some sources, however, do not receive a trustworthiness score as they are classified as a ``platform'' (63 domains, e.g., YouTube). In addition, the database includes 63 sources judged as ``satire'' (e.g., The Onion) and 454 lifestyle sources (e.g., fitworldnow.com). NewsGuard does not rate these sources, but they make up less than 1\% of the listed sources in the database. For the other 10,513 rated sources, the database includes the following types of ratings (see Table SX for a full list of variables):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unique identifier for each rating and the date of the last update
    \item Domain name and parent domain
    \item A trustworthiness rating as a continuous scale (between 0 and 100) and binary labels (N = Not Trustworthy/T = Trustworthy)
    \item Binary labels for nine journalistic quality criteria constituting the trustworthiness rating
    \item Language and country associated with the domain
    \item Political orientation of the outlet
    \item Topics covered (e.g., ``local news'', ``political news'', ``health information'') by the outlet
    \item Flags indicating whether the outlet frequently posts issues, narratives or AI-generated content
\end{itemize}

\section{Descriptive Analyses}
\subsection{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings?}
A trained team of NewsGuard experts regularly judges the trustworthiness of the selected news sources. In the following, we review how the database, and particularly the trustworthiness ratings and domain selection, have changed over the years.

\textbf{Coverage.} Over the span of four years, NewsGuard has grown from 2,615 to 10,513 rated sources, as shown in Figure ~\ref{fig:fig1}. We sampled the database in monthly intervals and chose the middle of the month for the snapshot. However, when we sampled from the second day of each month, we observed an irregular dip in the number of domains (see May 2022 in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}). We think an error caused this fluctuation, as the approximately 3,500 removed domains were added again two weeks later. In our case, changing the sampling procedure to mid-month shows to be more stable. Nevertheless, the substantive loss in the number of domains caused by one difference in the sampling strategy indicates that comparing multiple snapshots of the database can be decisive.

\textbf{Distribution.} NewsGuard trustworthiness ratings are assigned on a scale from 0 to 100, with 100 being trustworthy. The threshold for the trustworthiness of a source is set at 60. Notably, NewsGuard is not a blacklist. In fact, close to 60\% of domains have an overall rating of over 60 and are therefore considered ``trustworthy'' (see Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2} for the distribution of trustworthiness scores). When looking at the score distribution over time, the left skew diminishes slightly: while the average score was 70.79 (SD=35.33) in 2019, it is now at 64.61 (SD=32.33). In other words, the average trustworthiness of domains has decreased over time. The distributions of trustworthiness from 2019 to 2023 are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}.

\subsection{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?}

\textbf{Country coverage.} The dataset contains sources from nine countries in total (United States, Great Britain, Italy, Canada, France, Germany, Austria, Australia, and New Zealand) plus sources considered to be global. The majority of sources are from the United States (75.04\%), followed by global sources (14.05\%), Great Britain (6.4\%), Italy (5.28\%), Canada (4.91\%), France (3.8\%) and Germany (3.59\%), and Australia (1.3\%). The remaining countries, Austria and New Zealand, account for less than 1\% of sources each. NewsGuard also clusters sources based on language (English, Italian, French, German, Spanish). \subsection{How can other information be used for misinformation research?}
\textbf{Journalistic criteria.} The trustworthiness score is a composite score that is calculated based on nine journalistic criteria. For each domain, the organization assigns a binary label (yes/no) per criterion. The trustworthiness score is then calculated as the sum of the weighted criteria. The criteria are weighted differently, ordered in descending relevance:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'' (22 points)
  \item ``Gathers and presents information responsibly'' (18 points) 
  \item ``Regularly corrects or clarifies errors'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Handles the difference between news and opinion responsibly'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' (10 points)
  \item ``Website discloses ownership and financing' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Clearly labels advertising'' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Reveals who's in charge, including possible conflicts of interest'' (5 points)
  \item ``The site provides the names of content creators, along with either contact or biographical information'' (5 points)
\end{itemize}

Further explanation of the criteria and their weights can be found in the NewsGuard FAQ\footnote{\href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. 

In the latest version of the dataset, the criterion ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' was the most respected, and 8,597 of the sources satisfied it (78.9 \% of sources), followed by ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'' (77.5 \% of sources). Across all dataset versions, when one of the two criteria is satisfied, the other one tends to as well (Pearson's r = 0.88). A factor analysis with oblique rotation confirms that the two criteria make up one factor (accounting for 56\% of variance explained and with factor loadings > 0.9), while all other criteria share 35.9\% of the variance explained (factor loadings >.6). Meeting one of two criteria strongly predicts a high rating, and vice versa (with coefficients of 64.74,  <.001, and 67.28, p < .001, respectively). In line with that, 11.97\% of the ratings only meet these two criteria; the most common combination of criteria, however, is that all criteria are satisfied (17.97\% of ratings)––given that the majority of sources are considered trustworthy. 

Furthermore, looking at domain criteria updates, we find that most sources also keep their scores. The most common update pattern is a negative change in the criterion ``Website discloses ownership and financing'', occurring, for example, once for the German weekly newspaper zeit.de. This criterion appears to be the most volatile, with 13.26\% of sources eventually changing their label negatively. On the other hand, a positive update is also the third most common pattern (with 10.77\% of sources), indicating that criteria related to ownership might be a criterion that is frequently updated/monitored by NewsGuard. 

\textbf{Political Orientation.} The dataset also classifies sources according to their political orientation. The classification relies on a left-to-right conception of the political spectrum. In December 2022, the labels shifted from four (far left/ slightly left/slightly right/far right) to only two possible labels (left/right). 

Overall, the political orientation dimension covers only a minority of the sources in the database. In the current version of the database, only 30.76\% of the sources have a rating for political orientation (3,412 sources). The ratio decreases when zooming in on singular countries: only 38.73\% of news outlets have a political orientation rating in the United States, 10.08\% in Great Britain, 14.23\% in Italy, 17.6\% in Germany, and 22.63\% in France. Here, again, the United States is over-represented in the dataset. Table~\ref{table:table2} shows the ratings per country and the percentages of coverage per country. In most countries, the majority of sources with political orientation ratings are judged as right.

Trustworthiness ratings are generally lower for right-leaning sources. In the current database, the mean trustworthiness score for right-leaning sources is 26.39 (SD=19.93), while it is 66.28 (SD=22.45) for left-leaning sources. Averaging across all months and collapsing the ratings to left/right, the difference in trustworthiness increases (left: 71.96, 24.26; right: 27.30, 26.62). Before the rating system changed, left- and right-leaning sources had moderate trustworthiness ratings (for example, in June 2021, 82.16 and 61.9, respectively). In August 2021, however, the trustworthiness of right-leaning sources dropped by almost 23 points, see Figure~\ref{fig:fig6}. When the ratings changed to binary, this classification punishes right-leaning sources especially, with the trustworthiness of far-right sources ranging between 13.48 and 17.84 only.

\textbf{Topics.} The database assigns topic labels, which are based on the topics covered by the outlets. The topics are not mutually exclusive, i.e., a source can be assigned to multiple topics. The most popular topics in the database are ``Political News or Commentary'' (37.89\% of ratings), ``Conspiracy Theories or Hoaxes'' (17.93\%), and ``Health or medical information'' (17.24\%). The topic ratings are also not complete: Out of all ratings, 18,245 ratings (40.9\%) are not classified under any topic, but in the most recent version of the database, only 282 domains are without a topic (2.9\%). 

Trustworthiness ratings greatly differ across topics. The quality of sources linked to each topic is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fig8}. The topic trustworthiness, again, heavily depends on the context. For instance, for medical information, sources in Canada are rated as relatively trustworthy (M=64, SD=34.55). \textbf{Other content.}
NewsGuard also maintains ``Flags'' labels that were first added to the database in August 2020.
