\begin{abstract}
\noindent To study the spread of misinformation on digital platforms, it is imperative for computational social scientists to have access to reliable and valid tools for identifying misinformation. A prominent way to track misinformation is using a list of domains of news sources. The organization NewsGuard offers the most comprehensive database of domain trustworthiness ratings and is increasing in popularity despite restrictive access. Yet, the database was neither created as a research instrument nor has it ever been reviewed systematically for this purpose. Therefore, we provide a comprehensive description and inspection of the NewsGuard database across the variability of trustworthiness ratings over time, the selection of domains, and the completeness of news categories such as political orientation. By describing the database over time and across countries, we outline potential pitfalls and recommendations for digital media research and discuss appropriate use cases. 
\end{abstract}
\keywords{\textit{misinformation, news trustworthiness, NewsGuard} \vspace{8ex}}

\section{Introduction}
Misinformation permeates both social media platforms \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018} and mainstream media channels \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. However, only a fraction of news consumption is attributed to false news -- roughly ~0.15\% \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020}--and only a small group of users actively engage with unreliable information online \citep{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. Typically, these individuals hold strong political or ideological beliefs (REF) and accept and actively look for information that echoes their opinions (REF) -- information that often comes from established media sources (REF). Consequently, it is unsurprising that misinformation capitalizes on these dynamics to stoke conflict and evoke negative emotions, thereby exacerbating partisan divides and fueling inter-group hostility \citep{gonzalez-bailonAsymmetricIdeologicalSegregation2023a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. Against a backdrop of declining trust in both media outlets \citep{newmanReutersInstituteDigital2023} and institutions \citep{bennettDisinformationAge2020}, it becomes increasingly crucial to comprehend the mechanisms driving the dissemination of misinformation, particularly in the digital sphere. Digital media not only facilitate the measurement of these dynamics but also have the potential to reinforce polarization and misinformation (Lorenz-Spreen, 2022).
The spread of misinformation has been studied extensively by scholars from various scientific disciplines, relying on diverse conceptualizations. In psychology, researchers commonly define misinformation as inaccurate or unreliable information \citep{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022}, while in communication science, a popular definition by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan (\citeyear{wardleInformationDisorderInterdisciplinary2017}) distinguishes misinformation from disinformation and fake news that pursue financial or political intentions. Computational social scientists, for instance, David Lazer and colleagues (\citeyear{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}), follow this notion:
"Fake news overlaps with other information disorders, such as misinformation (false or misleading information) and disinformation (false information that is purposely spread to deceive people)." (p. 1094)

Inconsistent definitions of misinformation present researchers with numerous challenges, leaving them with a number of degrees of freedom. The task of defining the boundaries of misinformation, essentially deciding where to draw the line to the truth, is laden with complexity. In reality, these conceptual choices are often shaped by methodological constraints or disciplinary preferences, which is why terms like misinformation, fake news, false news, or rumors are often used interchangeably. Consequently, this semantic variance has contributed to imprecise or inconclusive findings regarding the prevalence and impact of misinformation. While some scholars, such as \citet{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a}, argue that the effects of misinformation are overstated, calling it a symptom of broader social issues, others, like \cite{tayThinkingClearlyMisinformation2024}, reason that the heterogeneity in measuring misinformation contributes to divergent evidence and creates a false dichotomy between understanding misinformation as a symptom or a cause.

To study the diffusion or discussion of misinformation on social media, computational researchers commonly opt for either story- or source-based methodologies. Both approaches rely on experts, such as fact-checkers, to curate a list of stories or sources, which serves as the foundation for collecting data from one or more digital platforms. These approaches, however, operate at different analytical levels, and the choice between them depends on the primary research objective. When the goal is to study specific instances of misinformation with high certainty, e.g., focusing on a specific case (EXAMPLE), the story-based approach proves advantageous. By selecting a handful of stories, researchers can ensure low variance in accuracy ratings and achieve high precision in capturing misinformation. For instance, Vosoughi et al. (2018) traced the diffusion of fact-checked fake news stories on Twitter (BETTER REF). Additionally, this method takes away the burden on researchers to judge the trustworthiness of individual stories, as experts have already determined their falsehood objectively. 

However, relying solely on a small selection of fact-checked stories has its limitations. Such stories, while clearly false and widely debated, represent only a fraction of misinformation present on social media platforms, and it is impossible to generalize findings beyond that specific, potentially biased case. Moreover, these forms of operationalization may not encapsulate the full spectrum of misinformation, and largely neglect more subtle (likely not fact-checked) and harder to detect forms of misinformation. Furthermore, the selection bias inherent in fact-checking organizations' coverage may skew the representation of misinformation, neglecting topics for which they lack expertise at a given time. Consequently, this approach tends to underestimate both the prevalence and impact of misinformation, failing to capture its broader dynamics effectively. 

On the other hand, source-based strategies offer the advantage of encompassing a broader spectrum of sources and narratives, thus enriching the sample with a more diverse information environment. By sampling based on sources, these approaches enable the inclusion of subtler forms of misinformation and facilitate accurate estimations of both the scale \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020, grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019,guessExposureUntrustworthyWebsites2020a} and dynamics of dissemination \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a, pennycookShiftingAttentionAccuracy2021a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. Nevertheless, it's important to note that not every story originating from an untrustworthy source is necessarily false, introducing a degree of inaccuracy at the individual article level. Despite potential errors in assessing accuracy at this granularity, source-based judgments of trustworthiness can remain unbiased at a macroscopic level. Even if unreliable publishers predominantly share unreliable news articles, they may intersperse them with unreliable opinion pieces or occasionally publish reliable news pieces to attract new (or keep existing) audiences.

The precision of source-based approaches hinges largely on the selection of sources within a given list. Strictly speaking, this conceptualization covers information disseminated by unreliable sources -- making it essential to recognize that observed patterns may be intricately linked not just to misinformation itself but also to the inherent biases or editorial standards of the source. This association underscores the importance of considering factors such as political bias or editorial practices \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}, arguing that a lack of editorial oversight can compromise the quality of news reporting. To mitigate the influence of other source-related variables, source-based approaches can incorporate assessments of source biases and misleading tendencies while maintaining generalizability. Nonetheless, in practice, source categorizations often oversimplify the nuanced spectrum of misinformation, relying on a binary classification system (fake/real). Moreover, it's crucial to acknowledge the temporal and contextual sensitivity of source ratings, as the quality of information from sources may fluctuate over time, potentially due to changes in ownership or evolving editorial standards. Additionally, while source judgments are typically made without requiring specific topical expertise, they often reflect the journalistic norms of a particular media ecosystem, which may not be universally applicable across different contexts.

Taken together, source-based approaches are a promising way to track misinformation online. However, for computational social scientists to study the spread of unreliable news, they need access to reliable and credible databases of source ratings. Since the 2016 U.S. elections, the most prominent way to track misinformation online has been a list of untrustworthy or unreliable domains \citep[e.g.,][]{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. The organization \href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/}{NewsGuard} offers the most comprehensive list. It does not just blacklist untrustworthy domains but provides a trustworthiness score for each domain, covering domains across the entire spectrum of news quality. A team of journalists and editors rate the domains based on nine journalistic quality criteria and regularly updates both the coverage of the list (increasing the number of domains) and their overall trustworthiness rating. The database is constantly growing and has recently been extended to countries beyond the United States. With recent political events and crises, demand for such databases has increased, enhancing the popularity of NewsGuard. For instance, it has been used to study user engagement with unreliable news during the 2016 and 2020 US elections \citet{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a} or to assess if Republican US Congress members have contributed to spreading misinformation by sharing links to untrustworthy sources \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a}. 

Importantly, however, the NewsGuard database was initially created as a tool for brand security rather than scientific research, which is also why access is paywalled and expensive. In light of the mentioned disadvantages of list-based approaches, we provide a comprehensive description of the content of this database. We aim to answer the following research question: How reliable is the NewsGuard database of domain trustworthiness ratings over time and language contexts? For this, we address the following aspects of the NewsGuard database in detail: First, we look into the distribution and update process of the trustworthiness ratings as well as the selection and removal of sources over time. Second, we assess the completeness of the database for different countries, and lastly, we estimate the usefulness of other information provided in the database, e.g., journalistic criteria and political orientation. Therefore, we focus on three questions:

\textbf{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings, e.g., how often do ratings change or do domains vanish?} 
First, we investigate the development of the NewsGuard database over time. This is important to estimate whether using an older database will distort results. We describe the different versions of the database and reproduce published research relying on the NewsGuard list with different versions of the list to assess the degree to which results depend on a specific version of the list. 

\textbf{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?} A recent study has shown high agreement of NewsGuard with other expert-rated lists in the U.S. context~\citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}, but an assessment for other countries and contexts is missing. Therefore, we next compare the distribution and completeness of information provided by NewsGuard across different contexts. 

\textbf{How can other information be used for misinformation research?} For each source, NewsGuard experts rate journalistic quality criteria, topics, and political orientation, but they also give sources flags for issues. Estimating to what extent the different criteria constitute the overall trustworthiness score and the completeness of additional information might shed light on aspects of misinformation beyond binary true/false labels.

\section{Dataset construction}
NewsGuard collects the list of sources to rate from tracking online activity. On their website, NewsGuard claims to have reviewed domains accounting for 95\% of online engagement. The organization also manually adds sources they deem influential or that changed domains. The database automatically updates every hour and allows us to track changes going back to March 2019, when the organization started operating. Still, the procedure by which domains are selected or removed is undisclosed on the website, as are the intervals in which the trustworthiness assessments are updated in the database. 

As of December 15, 2023, the database has 11,314 rating entries. Some sources, however, do not receive a trustworthiness score as they are classified as a ``platform'' (63 domains, e.g., YouTube). In addition, the database includes 63 sources judged as ``satire'' (e.g., The Onion) and 530 lifestyle sources (e.g., healthquotefree.com). NewsGuard does not rate these sources, but they make up 5.8\% of the listed sources in the database. For the other 10,658 ratings sources, the database includes the following types of ratings (see Table SX for a full list of variables):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unique identifier for each rating and the date of the last update
    \item Domain name and parent domain (e.g., nytimes.com)
    \item A trustworthiness rating as a continuous scale (between 0 and 100) and binary labels (N = Not Trustworthy/T = Trustworthy) based on thresholding the trustworthiness scale at 60
    \item Binary labels for nine journalistic quality criteria, each awarding a number of points which together constitute the overall trustworthiness rating
    \item Language and country associated with the domain
    \item Political orientation of the outlet
    \item Topics covered (e.g., ``local news'', ``political news'', ``health information'') by the outlet
    \item Flags indicating whether the outlet frequently posts certain issues or narratives.
\end{itemize}

\section{Descriptive Analyses}
\subsection{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings?}
A trained team of NewsGuard experts regularly judges the trustworthiness of the selected news sources based on nine journalistic criteria\footnote{\href{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}} that are treated globally for all sources. Therefore, the trustworthiness score is a composite score. In the following, we review how the database, and particularly the trustworthiness ratings and domain selection, have changed over the years.

\textbf{Coverage.} Over the span of four years, NewsGuard has grown from 2,615 to 10,658 sources with ratings, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}. NewsGuard regularly adds new sources to the database but rarely removes any, i.e., the database has been growing to three times its initial size. When comparing the added domains for one month to the previous one, NewsGuard has added 7,937 sources over time while they only removed 632. More precisely, the NewsGuard team adds new sources every month, with an average of 144 domains per month.

NewsGuard ratings are made accessible to customers in an online database (Amazon S3 bucket), where a current snapshot of the rating database is uploaded every hour. We sampled the snapshot database in monthly intervals, choosing a snapshot from the first hour of the 15th of every month. However, when we sampled from the second day of each month instead, we observed an irregular dip in the number of domains (see May 2022 in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}). We think an error caused this fluctuation, as the approximately 3,500 removed domains were added again two weeks later. This sudden disappearance and reappearance of domains illustrate the overall volatility of the database. We therefore advise researchers to inspect several database snapshots and check their consistency before choosing which one to use.

The most recent database contains ratings for 9,946 unique domains; in other words, some domains appear multiple times. Typically, these are sources that publish in different languages, i.e., they have the same domain but provide multi-lingual content, or they are also considered a global source. For instance, the Austrian website servustv.com produces both German- and English-language output. Therefore, it appears twice in the database: Once with the country label ``AT'' and once with ``ALL''. Except for three such cases, the majority of duplicated domains have identical trustworthiness ratings, though. Therefore, dropping duplicated domain entries does not substantially affect downstream research results. However, the multi-lingual domain coverage could be especially useful for comparative research. In these use cases, duplicated domains with different country labels should be kept.

In addition, 5,186 of the rated domains belong to only 403 unique parent domains. Some parent domains have multiple variations of the domain, e.g., theragingpatriot.org has domains ending with .blog, .com, .net, .com, .pro, .net. other parent domains have very different domains, e.g., 24usnews.com and africdaily.com belong to charmdaily.com. The other 6,128 ratings do not have a parent domain entry in the database. 

\textbf{Distribution.} NewsGuard trustworthiness ratings are assigned on a scale from 0 to 100. Historically, NewsGuard considered sources with a rating below 60 as not trustworthy, therefore assigning binary trustworthiness labels\footnote{Historic snapshot of the description of rating process from December 31st, 2022 \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. Since February 2023\footnote{Historic snapshot of the changed rating page from February 1st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}, NewsGuard suggests a more granular categorization, classifying sources with a score of 100 as "high credibility", scores between 75 and 99 as "generally credible", scores between 60 and 74 as "credible with exceptions", scores between 40 and 59 as "proceed with caution", and scores between 0 and 39 as "proceed with maximum caution". Notably, NewsGuard is not a blacklist. In fact, close to 60\% of domains have an overall rating of over 60 (see Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2} for the distribution of trustworthiness scores). 

When looking at the score distribution over time, the left skew diminishes slightly: while the average score was 71.8 (SD=33.3) in 2019, it is now at 64.5 (SD=32.4). In other words, the average trustworthiness of domains contained in the database has decreased over time. The distributions of trustworthiness from 2019 to 2023 are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}. Given that the database tripled in size and that added sources tend to have a lower trustworthiness score (on average 61.8, SD=27.7), this development might explain the decreasing trustworthiness scores over time. Especially in 2020, a great number of sources with lower ratings were added to the database. The highest number of sources added to the database was in September 2020, when 1,030 sources with low trustworthiness (M=35.3, SD=14.1) were added all at once. It is impossible to say whether this development is due to a sampling bias towards untrustworthy news or a global change in news trustworthiness.

NewsGuard employs different teams of editors per country, potentially leading to country differences in coverage as well as ratings. For instance, dips in trustworthiness may be related to country-specific domains being added to the database (e.g., 1,015 of the 1,030 added sources in September 2020 are coded as U.S.). However, the addition of countries to the database has little effect on the trustworthiness scores. Overall, smaller dips appear across all countries covered and are most likely related to many low-quality sources popping up around the time of the COVID-19 pandemic (see Figure~\ref{fig:figa1}). 

Another explanation for the decrease in trustworthiness scores could be updates of existing ratings. NewsGuard indicates the date when a rating was last updated, even if the score did not change. Updates to entries happen on average once every eight months (243 days), but this varies strongly per domain, ranging from a few seconds to a maximum of 1,109 days between updates (but with an interquartile range from 162 to 355 days). When updated, trustworthiness scores mostly remain untouched, i.e., the majority of sources have not decreased in trustworthiness. However, a few domains drastically dropped in trustworthiness; for instance, conservativedailynews.com lost 80 points in trustworthiness in one update in November 2020 (see Figure~\ref{fig:figa2} that illustrates the differences in consecutive scores over time, with major score updates in early 2020). Overall, the decrease in trustworthiness that we can see in the right-hand plot in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2} can, therefore, likely be attributed to NewsGuard adding untrustworthy sources rather than previously trustworthy domains losing their reliability. 

\textbf{Composition of trustworthiness score.} 
The trustworthiness score is a composite score that is calculated based on nine journalistic criteria. For each domain, the organization assigns a Boolean value (yes/no) per criterion. The trustworthiness score is then calculated as the sum of the weighted criteria. Because of the discrete point contributions to the overall score, the resulting trustworthiness score has a limited number of values it can take based on the different combinations of categories. The criteria are weighted differently, ordered in descending relevance:

\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'', changed to ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' in December 2023 (22 points)
  \item ``Gathers and presents information responsibly'' (18 points) 
  \item ``Regularly corrects or clarifies errors'', changed to ``Has effective practices for correcting errors''(12.5 points)
  \item ``Handles the difference between news and opinion responsibly'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' (10 points)
  \item ``Website discloses ownership and financing' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Clearly labels advertising'' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Reveals who's in charge, including possible conflicts of interest'' (5 points)
  \item ``The site provides the names of content creators, along with either contact or biographical information'' (5 points)
\end{itemize}

Further explanation of the criteria and their weights can be found in the NewsGuard FAQ\footnote{Here taken from December 31st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. 

In the latest version of the dataset, the criterion ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' was the most respected, and 8,903 of the sources satisfied it (83.6\% of sources), followed by ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' (82\% of sources). When one of the two criteria is satisfied, the other one tends to as well (Pearson's r = 0.88). A factor analysis with oblique rotation confirms that the two criteria make up one factor (accounting for 56.4\% of variance explained and with factor loadings > 0.9), while all other criteria share 36.5\% of the variance explained (factor loadings >.6). Meeting one of two criteria strongly predicts a high rating, and vice versa (with coefficients of 61.91, p <.001, and 61.94, p < .001, respectively). In line with that, 13.5\% of the ratings only meet these two criteria, while 10.2\% of the sources satisfy none of the criteria. The most common combination of criteria, however, is that all criteria are satisfied (15.7\% of ratings)––given that the majority of sources are considered trustworthy. A correlation matrix of criteria can be found in the Appendix (Table~\ref{table:tablea1}.

Furthermore, looking at domain criteria updates over time, we find that most sources also keep their scores. The most common update pattern is a negative change in the criterion ``Website discloses ownership and financing'', occurring, for example, once for the German weekly newspaper zeit.de. This criterion appears to be the most volatile, with 12\% of sources eventually changing their label negatively. On the other hand, a positive update is also the third most common pattern (with 8.6\% of sources), indicating that criteria related to ownership might be a criterion that is frequently updated/monitored by NewsGuard. Update patterns of singular criteria over time are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}.

\subsection{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?}
\textbf{Country coverage.} The dataset (2019-2023) contains sources from nine countries in total (United States, Great Britain, Italy, Canada, France, Germany, Austria, Australia, and New Zealand) plus sources considered to be global. Countries were added in the following order: GB, US, ALL, IT, FR, DE (all in 2019), CA (2021), AU, AT (2022), and NZ (2023). 

The majority of sources are from the United States (76\%), followed by global sources (13.8\%). Table~\ref{table:table2} gives an overview of the total number of unique sources covered per country and their average trustworthiness scores. NewsGuard also clusters sources based on language, with English being the most represented (8,417 sources; 88.1\%), followed by Italian (4.8\%), French (3.7\%), and German (3.5\%).

Across all versions of the database and most countries covered by NewsGuard, the overall trustworthiness of sources remains >65, except for the United States: U.S.-based outlets are of lower quality on average (see Table~\ref{table:table2}). Figure~\ref{fig:fig4} shows the average trustworthiness of sources per country over time. The development of trustworthiness indicates that for countries with good coverage (a higher number of sources), the database seems to have settled into a stable state, whereas for newer countries (CA, NZ, AU), rating coverage seems to vary. It is unclear whether U.S. sources are of lower trustworthiness overall or if this finding can be explained by the relative number of outlets in the dataset. 

When comparing the number of sources that meet the journalistic criteria as rated by NewsGuard per country, we observe some striking patterns. Table~\ref{table:table3} shows the percentage of sources that miss a given indicator per country, i.e., a high percentage indicates that many sources don't meet a criterion. For most countries, more than 50\% of sources meet each criterion. Interestingly, almost 80\% of Italian sources don't provide names of content creators, and over 70\% don't effectively correct errors. A similar but less pronounced pattern is visible for French sources. Across all criteria, the United States shows the highest percentage of sources not meeting the criteria, indicating that lower trustworthiness scores in Figure~\ref{fig:fig4} and Table~\ref{table:table2} seem to result from a high number of sources not meeting journalistic quality criteria as defined by NewsGuard, as well as a high number of sources not meeting the two criteria with the biggest weights (18.8\% for ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' and 47.5\% for ``Gathers and presents information responsible'').

Due to the size of the database per country, the U.S. database was updated on average 6,306 updates per year (37,839 times in total, based on our sampling strategy), while the maximum number of times that the second most comprehensive database, Italy, was updated is only 663. Times between updates of sources vary substantially (see~\ref{table:table2}, ranging from 235 days (New Zealand), 262 days (U.S.) to 399 days (Germany). Updating patterns do not seem to depend on the country. Rather, domains seem to get updated once a year per country. Overall, however, the U.S. coverage seems the most complete and reliable, given the total number of outlets rated by NewsGuard.

\textbf{Comparison with other lists.} In order to measure the true coverage of sources per country, it is necessary to compare the sources covered by NewsGuard with other lists. However, other lists are often not compiled with the same objective, here, to cover all sources responsible for most of the traffic. Therefore, their scope and rating system may differ. In addition, other lists are not available for every country. 

For the U.S. context, ~\citet{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023} have compared five lists of existing expert ratings with NewsGuard and found a high number of non-overlapping domains, while the quality ratings largely overlapped. Further, Puschmann et al. (2024) collected a list of German online news domains (GOND)\footnote{\href{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}}, largely relying on web tracking data of a representative sample of 1,500 German citizens. In the first version of the list, the authors also classified the domains into types, among others ``legacy press'' (49\%), ``digital-born news outlet'' (18.0\%), or ``hyperpartisan news'' (8.2\%). GOND shares 543 global sources with NewsGuard, with 147 English-speaking, 47 French, and 56 Italian sources. Out of all global sources that overlap, 285 are legacy press and 56 are hyperpartisan news. Furthermore, GOND covers the same 237 German sources as NewsGuard, including 24 hyperpartisan and 19 digital-born news outlets. Overall, it seems that NewsGuard coverage for the United States and Germany are extensive, while comparisons for other countries are missing.

\subsection{How can other information be used for misinformation research?}
\textbf{Political Orientation.} The dataset also classifies sources according to their political orientation. The classification relies on a left-to-right conception of the political spectrum. In December 2022, the labels shifted from four (far left/ slightly left/slightly right/far right) to only two possible labels (left/right). 

Overall, the political orientation dimension covers only a minority of the sources in the database. In the current version of the database, 31.2\% of the sources have a rating for political orientation (2,980 sources). The ratio decreases when zooming in on individual countries: 41.1\% of news outlets have a political orientation rating in the United States, whereas only 9.8\% have such a rating in Great Britain, 11.6\% in Italy, 16.1\% in Germany, and 15.3\% in France. Here, again, the over-representation of the United States in the data set becomes apparent. Table~\ref{table:table4} shows the ratings per country and the coverage per country in terms of the percentage of sources in the most recent database that have a political orientation rating. In most countries, the majority of sources with political orientation ratings are judged as belonging to the right-wing spectrum. 

%\input{tables/t4_orientation_countries.tex}

Trustworthiness ratings are generally lower for right-leaning sources. In the current database (December 2023), the mean trustworthiness score for right-leaning sources is 26.7 (SD=23.4), while it is 61.7 (SD=20.1) for left-leaning sources, Mdiff=35.0, t(2978)=32.4, p < .001. Before the political orientation rating system changed, left- and right-leaning sources had moderate trustworthiness ratings (for example, in June 2021, 82.16 and 61.9, respectively) in contrast to the far left and far right sources. In August/September 2021, however, when a high number of untrustworthy sources were added to the database, particularly the trustworthiness of right-leaning sources dropped by almost 23 points, see Figure~\ref{fig:fig6}. When the ratings changed to left/right, this classification also to a lower average score of right-leaning sources by adding low-quality sources from what was classified as "far right" before (where trustworthiness ranged between 13.5 and 17.8 points).

\textbf{Topics.} The database assigns topic labels, which are based on the topics covered by the outlets. The topics are not mutually exclusive, i.e., a source is usually assigned to multiple topics. The topic ratings were introduced around October 2020 and are incomplete for older versions of the database. In the most recent version of the database, only 146 sources are without a topic rating (1.7\%). The most popular topics are ``Local News'' (39.1\%), ``Political News or Commentary'' (37.9\%), ``General News'' (19.9\%), ``Health or medical information'' (10.5\%), and ``Conspiracy Theories or Hoaxes'' (9.5\%). 

Up until 2022, fewer topic labels were given (see Fig.~\ref{fig:figa3}), but throughout the covered time frame, the most popular topics remained the same. Especially health-related information, as well as political news, peaks at least once a year, most likely coinciding with update intervals. Interestingly, only about a year after the COVID-19 pandemic started, in August 2021, 1,468 labels related to COVID-19 misinformation were added to the database. This update coincides with a drop in trustworthiness for right-wing sources, as described above.

Trustworthiness ratings greatly differ across topics. Sources covering issues related to ``Military and Defense'' have the lowest average rating (M=58.3, SD=39.6), while those covering ``Education'' (M=90.2, SD=10.9), ``Business or Personal Finance'' (M=87.5, SD=2.3), and ``General News'' (M=83.3, SD=14.3) have the highest average trustworthiness scores.

\section{Conclusion}
Main findings:
Overall, more than half of the rated news is considered trustworthy. 
The skewed distribution of scores reflects the presence of misinformation on the right-wing spectrum. Does that mean that there are more news outlets on the right spectrum, or does NewsGuard have a bias?
The database gets updated at least once a year.


Implications:
Using a monthly snapshot of the database should be enough to cover changes
Always consider the country context: English-speaking countries (U.S. and U.K.) coverage is great; Italy, France, and Germany depend.

Limitations:
Misses user-created misinformation
