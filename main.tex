\begin{abstract}
\noindent To study the spread of misinformation on digital platforms, it is imperative for computational social scientists to have access to reliable and valid tools that enable them to identify misinformation. A prominent way to track misinformation is to use a list of domains. For example, the organization NewsGuard offers the most comprehensive database of domain trustworthiness ratings and is increasing in popularity. Despite restrictive access, the database was never created as a research instrument and has never been reviewed systematically. Therefore, we offer a comprehensive description and inspection of the NewsGuard database, across the following dimensions: the reliability of trustworthiness ratings, the selection of domains, as well as the completeness of categories such as political orientation. By describing the database, we outline potential pitfalls and recommendations for digital media research and discuss appropriate use cases. 
\end{abstract}
\keywords{\textit{misinformation, NewsGuard, social media} \vspace{8ex}}

\section{Introduction}
Misinformation permeates both social media platforms \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018} and mainstream media channels \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. Despite only a fraction of news consumption being attributed to false news — roughly ~0.15\% \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020} — and only a small group of users actively engaging with unreliable information online \citep{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. Typically, these individuals hold strong political or ideological beliefs, seeking information that echoes their opinions. Consequently, it is unsurprising that misinformation exacerbates partisan divides and fuels inter-group hostility \citep{gonzalez-bailonAsymmetricIdeologicalSegregation2023a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}, capitalizing on these dynamics to stoke conflict and evoke negative emotions. Because of decreasing levels of trust (and other consequences), it has become more critical to understand such dynamics reinforcing the dissemination of misinformation and their relationship with digital media. 

The spread of misinformation has been studied extensively by scholars from various scientific disciplines, relying on diverse conceptualizations. In psychology, researchers commonly define misinformation as inaccurate or unreliable information \citep{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022}, while in communication science, a common definition is that by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan (\citeyear{wardleInformationDisorderInterdisciplinary2017}), distinguishing misinformation from disinformation and fake news that pursues financial or political intentions. A definition by computational social scientist David Lazer and colleagues (\citeyear{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}) follows this notion:
"Fake news overlaps with other information disorders, such as misinformation (false or misleading information) and disinformation (false information that is purposely spread to deceive people)." \cite[p. 1094]{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}
The conceptual choice leaves researchers with certain degrees of freedom, mostly concerning the boundaries of misinformation (the degree of deception). In reality, however, the definition of misinformation largely depends on methodological constraints or publication bias, and the terms are often used interchangeably. Consequences are imprecise or inconclusive findings around the prevalence of misinformation and its effects. While \citet{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a} makes the observation that misinformation is overstated \cite{tayThinkingClearlyMisinformation2024} argues that heterogeneity in how we measure misinformation results in heterogeneity in evidence and creates a false dichotomy of understanding misinformation as a symptom or a cause.

To study the diffusion or discussion of misinformation on social media, computational researchers commonly follow a selection of stories or sources. Focusing on stories or a source changes the level of analysis. Both approaches typically rely on experts, such as fact-checkers, to curate a list that is then used as a starting point to collect data from one or more digital platforms. The choice between story- and source-based approaches hinges on the primary research objective: When operating at the level of the story, we capture misinformation with high precision, but the selection of stories can be narrow and is likely biased. For instance, selecting a few fact-checked fake news stories is easy for researchers, and the stories are objectively false \citep{vosoughiSpreadTrueFalse2018a}. Here, the researcher does not have to judge the trustworthiness of the stories themselves. On the other hand, they picked stories that only represent a small proportion of misinformation and disregarded other topics or subtle misinformation that is presented more seriously. This approach surely captures dynamics related to some types of misinformation, but their estimate could also be off or underestimate both the reach of misinformation and the effects thereof. 

Conversely, with a source-based strategy \citep{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}, we can cover a wider variety of sources and stories, and hence, the data may be less biased. By focusing on a wider variety of sources, for example, we include more subtle types of misinformation and assess both the scale and dynamics of its spread. However, the latter, source-based approach risks overgeneralizing across stories, and source ratings may be especially sensitive to time and context. With this approach, we risk finding patterns that are not so much related to misinformation but to the political bias of the source or other editorial practices. While a lack of editorial practices may lead to lower quality in news stories \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}, the trustworthiness of news stories may vary. A lot of times, categorizations of sources are made on a binary scale, neglecting more subtle forms of misinformation. 

In conclusion, for computational social scientists to study the spread of unreliable news, they require access to reliable and credible data. A prominent way to track misinformation is to use a list of untrustworthy domains (e.g.,). The organization \href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/}{NewsGuard} offers the most comprehensive database, not just blacklisting domains but providing a trustworthiness score per domain. A team of journalists and editors rate the domains based on nine journalistic quality criteria and regularly updates both the coverage of the list, i.e., the number of domains, as well as their overall trustworthiness rating. Recently, the database has been extended to other countries than the United States and is constantly growing. Despite increasing popularity~\citep[for example,][]{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a,lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a}, however, the database was created as a tool for brand security rather than research, which is why access is paywalled and expensive. In order to provide a comprehensive description of the content of this database, we introduce our research question: How reliable is the NewsGuard database of domain trustworthiness ratings over time and language contexts? 

More precisely, we believe that it is essential to address the following aspects of the database in more detail: the distribution, construction, weighting, and update process of the ratings, the selection and removal of domains, and the completeness and reliability of other information provided in the database, e.g., political orientation. Therefore, we focus on three questions:

\textbf{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings, e.g., how often do ratings change or do domains vanish?} 
This is important to estimate whether using an older database will distort results. We reproduce published research relying on the NewsGuard list with different versions of the list to assess the degree to which results depend on a specific version of the list. %add more info here
  
\textbf{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?} A recent study has shown high agreement of NewsGuard with other expert-rated lists in the U.S. context~\citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}, but an assessment for other countries and contexts is missing. 

\textbf{How can other information be used for misinformation research?} For each domain, NewsGuard experts rate journalistic quality criteria, topics, and political orientation, but they also label AI-generated content. Estimating to what extent the different criteria constitute the overall trustworthiness score and the completeness of additional information might shed light on aspects of misinformation beyond binary true/false labels.

By describing the database, we outline potential pitfalls for digital media research and offer recommendations for the application of the NewsGuard database in different contexts. %short summary of findings
We also want to empower misinformation researchers to make informed decisions about whether the substantial expense associated with acquiring access to NewsGuard is necessary for their research. 

\section{Dataset construction}
As of November 15, 2023, the database contains 11,093 trustworthiness assessments. A trained team of NewsGuard experts regularly assesses the trustworthiness of the selected news sources. The organization also manually adds sources they deem influential or that changed domains. The database automatically updates every hour and allows us to track changes going back to March 2019, when the organization started operating. On their website, NewsGuard claims to have reviewed domains accounting for 95\% of online engagement. Still, the procedure by which domains are selected or removed is undisclosed on the website, as are the intervals in which the trustworthiness assessments are updated in the database. 
Some sources, however, do not receive a trustworthiness score as they are classified as a ``platform'' (63 domains, e.g., YouTube). In addition, the database includes 63 sources judged as ``satire'' (e.g., The Onion) and 454 lifestyle sources (e.g., fitworldnow.com). NewsGuard does not rate these sources, but they make up less than 1\% of the listed sources in the database. We exclude them from further analysis. For the other 10,513 rated sources, the database includes the following types of ratings (see Table SX for a full list of variables):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Domain and trustworthiness rating (between 0 and 100).
    \item Binary labels for nine journalistic quality criteria constituting the trustworthiness rating. 
    \item Language and country associated with the domain.
    \item Political orientation of the outlet.
    \item Topics covered (e.g., ``local news'', ``political news'', ``health information'') by the outlet
    \item A flag indicating whether the outlet frequently posts AI-generated content.
\end{itemize}

Over the span of four years, NewsGuard has grown from 2,615 to 10,513 rated sources, as shown in Figure ~\ref{fig:fig1}. We sampled the database in monthly intervals and chose the middle of the month for the snapshot. However, when we sampled from the second day of each month, we observed an irregular dip in the number of domains (see May 2022 in Figure ~\ref{fig:fig1}. We think an error caused this fluctuation, as the approximately 3,500 removed domains were added again two weeks later (see Appendix). In our case, changing the sampling procedure to mid-month shows to be more stable. Nevertheless, the substantive loss in the number of domains caused by one difference in the sampling strategy indicates that comparing multiple snapshots of the database can be decisive.

\section{Descriptive Analyses}
\subsection{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings?}
In the following, we review the distribution, construction, weighting, and update process of the trustworthiness ratings over the years. 

\textbf{Distribution.} NewsGuard trustworthiness ratings are assigned on a scale from 0 to 100, with 100 being trustworthy. The threshold for the trustworthiness of a source is set at 60. Notably, NewsGuard is not a blacklist. In fact, close to 60\% of domains have an overall rating of over 60 and are therefore considered ``trustworthy''(see Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2}) for the distribution of trustworthiness scores. 
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f2-trustworthiness_distribution.png}
    \caption{Distribution of domain trustworthiness contained in the NewsGuard database as of November 15, 2023.}
    \label{fig:fig2}
\end{figure}

When looking at the score distribution over time, the right skew has been diminishing; while the average score was 71.77 (SD=33.29) in 2019, it is now 64.54 (SD=32.44). In other words, the average trustworthiness of domains has decreased over time. The distributions of trustworthiness from 2019 to 2023 are shown in Figure ~\ref{fig:fig3}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f3-scores_over_time.png}
    \caption{Distribution of trustworthiness scores over time}
    \label{fig:fig3}
\end{figure}

NewsGuard regularly adds new sources to the database but rarely removes any, i.e., the database has been growing. When comparing the unique domains for one month to the previous month, NewsGuard has added 7,705 sources over time while they only removed 612. Removed sources tend to have a slightly lower trustworthiness score (M=53.50, SD=31.60) than added sources (M=62.09, SD=32.14). A great number of sources with lower ratings (M=46.5, SD=31.62) were added in 2020, which might explain the drop in trustworthiness in the following. 

For each entry in the database, NewsGuard indicates the date when it was last updated. Updates happen in regular intervals (on average every 20 days), but this varies strongly per domain, with a maximum of 192 days. When updated, trustworthiness scores mostly remain untouched, i.e., most sources have not decreased in trustworthiness. However, some domains drastically dropped in trustworthiness; for instance, conservativedailynews.com lost 80 points in trustworthiness in one update. Figure~\ref{fig:fig4} illustrates the differences in consecutive scores over time, with major score updates in early 2020. 

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f4-score_updates.png}
    \caption{Changes in trustworthiness scores over time, calculated as the difference in scores per domain between consecutive updates.}
    \label{fig:fig4}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Construction.} The trustworthiness score is a composite score that is calculated based on nine journalistic criteria. For each domain, the organization assigns a binary label (yes/no) per criterion. The trustworthiness score is then calculated as the sum of the weighted criteria. The criteria are weighted differently, ordered in descending relevance:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'' (22 points)
  \item ``Gathers and presents information responsibly'' (18 points) 
  \item ``Regularly corrects or clarifies errors'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Handles the difference between news and opinion responsibly'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' (10 points)
  \item ``Website discloses ownership and financing' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Clearly labels advertising'' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Reveals who's in charge, including possible conflicts of interest'' (5 points)
  \item ``The site provides the names of content creators, along with either contact or biographical information'' (5 points)
\end{itemize}

Further explanation of the criteria and their weights can be found in the NewsGuard FAQ\footnote{\href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. 

In the latest version of the dataset, the criterion ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' was the most respected, and 8,597 of the sources satisfied it (78.9 \% of sources), followed by ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'' (77.5 \% of sources). Across all dataset versions, when one of the two criteria is satisfied, the other one tends to as well ( Pearson's r = 0.88). A factor analysis with oblique rotation confirms that the two criteria make up one factor (accounting for 56\% of variance explained and with factor loadings > 0.9), while all other criteria share 35.9\% of the variance explained (factor loadings >.6). Meeting one of two criteria strongly predicts a high rating, and vice versa (with coefficients of 64.74,  <.001, and 67.28, p < .001, respectively). In line with that, 11.97\% of the ratings only meet these two criteria; the most common combination of criteria, however, is that all criteria are satisfied (17.97\% of ratings)––given that the majority of sources are considered trustworthy. 

Furthermore, looking at domain criteria updates, we find that most sources also keep their scores. However, the most common update pattern is a negative change in the criterion ``Website discloses ownership and financing'', occurring, for example, once for the German weekly newspaper zeit.de. This criterion appears to be the most volatile, with 13.26\% of sources eventually changing their label negatively. On the other hand, a positive update is also the third most common pattern (with 10.77\% of sources), indicating that it might be a criterion that is frequently updated/monitored by NewsGuard.

\subsection{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?}
The dataset contains sources from nine countries in total (United States, Great Britain, Italy, Canada, France, Germany, Austria, Australia, and New Zealand) plus sources considered to be global. The remaining countries, Austria and New Zealand, account for less than 1\% of sources each. The dataset also clusters sources based on language (English, Italian, French, German, Spanish). \subsection{How can other information be used for misinformation research?}
\textbf{Political Orientation}
The dataset also classifies sources according to their political orientation. The classification relies on a left-to-right conception of the political spectrum and should be interpreted in the context of the respective political landscape. Over the years, the labels have shifted from four to only two possible labels (left/right). 

%%completeness
Overall, the political orientation dimension covers only a minority of the sources in the database. Out of 9,811, 3,343 sources have a political orientation rating. The ratio decreases when zooming in on singular countries: only 42.87\% of news outlets have a political orientation rating in the United Stated, 14.57\% in Great Britain, 16.49\% in Italy, 19.74\% in Germany, 4.92\% in Canada, and 10\% in New Zealand. Here, again the United States is overrepresented in the dataset.

%%distributions 
Trustworthiness ratings are lower for right-leaning sources. The mean trustworthiness score for right-leaning sources is 58.5, while it is 66.5 for left-leaning sources. The median is 65 for right-leaning sources and 77.5 for left-leaning sources. The distribution of scores is shown in Figure X.

\textbf{Topics}
The database assigns topic labels, which are based on the topics covered by the outlets. The topics are not mutually exclusive, i.e., a source can be assigned to multiple topics. The topics are, for instance, ``Local News'', ``Political News or Commentary'', or ``Conspiracy Theories or Hoaxes''.
The topics are also not exhaustive, as 1,366 sources are not classified under any topic (see Table for topic occurence). 

Trustworthiness ratings greatly differ across topics. Figure X shows that sources covering conspiracies have the lowest average rating, while those covering ``Technology'' (M=87.16) and ``Business or Personal Finance'' (M=83.21) have the highest average ratings. The category ``Political News and Commentary'' attracts the highes share of bad sources: 80.09\% of all untrustworthy sources are said to cover that topic, making up the 76\% of all sources covering that topic.

The ``Conspiracy'' label contains only 39\% of the untrustworthy sources, suggesting that it only makes up a small proportion of all untrustworthy information. Nonetheless, it is a highly typical topics for bad sources: 99\% of the sources connected to this topic are untrustworthy. the quality of sources linked to each topic is illustrated in Figure X.
