\begin{abstract}
\noindent To study the spread of misinformation on digital platforms, it is imperative for computational social scientists to have access to reliable and valid tools for identifying misinformation. A prominent way to track misinformation is using a list of domains of news sources. The organization NewsGuard offers the most comprehensive database of domain trustworthiness ratings and is increasing in popularity despite restrictive access. Yet, the database was neither created as a research instrument nor has it ever been reviewed systematically for this purpose. Therefore, we provide a comprehensive description and inspection of the NewsGuard database, across the variability of trustworthiness ratings over time, the selection of domains, and the completeness of news categories such as political orientation. By describing the database over time and across countries, we outline potential pitfalls and recommendations for digital media research and discuss appropriate use cases. 
\end{abstract}
\keywords{\textit{misinformation, news trustworthiness, NewsGuard} \vspace{8ex}}

\section{Introduction}
Misinformation permeates both social media platforms \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018} and mainstream media channels \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. However, only a fraction of news consumption is attributed to false news -- roughly ~0.15\% \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020}--and only a small group of users actively engage with unreliable information online \citep{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. Typically, these individuals hold strong political or ideological beliefs, accept and actively look for information that echoes their opinions, and often come from established sources. Consequently, it is unsurprising that misinformation capitalizes on these dynamics to stoke conflict and evoke negative emotions, and thereby exacerbating partisan divides and fueling inter-group hostility \citep{gonzalez-bailonAsymmetricIdeologicalSegregation2023a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. With decreasing levels of trust in the media (Newman et al., 2020) and institutions (Bennett \& Livingston, 2020), it has become more critical to understand the dynamics reinforcing the dissemination of misinformation, in particular in digital media. Digital media do not only provide a means of making these dynamics measurable, but may potentially even accelerate polarization (Lorenz-Spreen, 2022). 

The spread of misinformation has been studied extensively by scholars from various scientific disciplines, relying on diverse conceptualizations. In psychology, researchers commonly define misinformation as inaccurate or unreliable information \citep{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022}, while in communication science, a popular definition is that by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan (\citeyear{wardleInformationDisorderInterdisciplinary2017}), distinguishing misinformation from disinformation and fake news that pursues financial or political intentions. Computational social scientists, for instance, David Lazer and colleagues (\citeyear{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}), follow this notion:
"Fake news overlaps with other information disorders, such as misinformation (false or misleading information) and disinformation (false information that is purposely spread to deceive people)." (p. 1094)

Inconsistent definitions leave researchers with a number of degrees of freedom. Here, researchers themselves can define the boundaries of misinformation, or in other words, they can decide where to draw the line on truth. In reality, such conceptual choices largely depend on methodological constraints or preferences by journals or conferences, which is why the terms are often used interchangeably. This has led to imprecise or inconclusive findings about the prevalence of misinformation and its effects. While \citet{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a} and many others make the observation that the effects of misinformation are overstated and advertise it as a symptom of social causes, \cite{tayThinkingClearlyMisinformation2024} argues that heterogeneity in how we measure misinformation explains this heterogeneity in evidence and creates a false dichotomy of understanding misinformation as a symptom or a cause.

To study the diffusion or discussion of misinformation on social media, computational researchers commonly follow a selection of stories or sources, thereby investigating the phenomenon at different levels of analysis. Both approaches typically rely on experts, such as fact-checkers, to curate a list that is then used as a starting point to collect data from one or more digital platforms. The choice between story- and source-based approaches hinges on the primary research objective: When operating at the level of the story, we find low variance in accuracy ratings, and we can capture misinformation with high precision, i.e., we can be sure that what we measure is related to false information. However, the selection of stories can be narrow, and it is nearly impossible to generalize findings beyond a specific, potentially biased case. \citet{vosoughiSpreadTrueFalse2018a}, for example, selected fact-checked fake news stories and followed their diffusion on Twitter. This approach has the advantage that researchers can rely on experts to determine what is objectively false; the researcher does not have to judge the trustworthiness of the stories themselves. On the other hand, misinformation taken from fact-checking websites is common and often widely debated. A lot of times, fact-checking organizations cover topics that they have experts for at that time. Therefore, the sample only represents a small proportion of misinformation and neglects more subtle types of misinformation that are harder to identify. While the story-level approach captures dynamics related to some types of misinformation, results tend to underestimate both the reach of misinformation and the effects thereof. %%add more example refs

Conversely, source-based strategies can cover a wider variety of sources and stories, and hence, samples scale up to a more diverse information environment. By sampling based on sources, we can include more subtle types of misinformation and get accurate estimates
both for the scale \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020, grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019,guessExposureUntrustworthyWebsites2020a} and dynamics of its spread \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a, pennycookShiftingAttentionAccuracy2021a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. But even in a case where we overgeneralize across articles and the error rate for the article accuracy is high, source judgments can still be unbiased at the macro level. Unreliable publishers publish mostly unreliable news pieces, but they may intentionally post reliable news pieces once in a while to attract new readers. The precision of such approaches depends on the selection of sources, but research has shown that lists for U.S.-based sources largely overlap \citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}. Now, if we are conceptually rigorous, misinformation in this context is defined by unreliable sources, and discernible patterns may be more closely associated with the source itself, including its political bias or editorial practices, rather than solely with misinformation. Indeed, a lack of editorial practices may lead to lower quality in news stories \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}. In order to exclude other source-related factors, this approach can take source biases and misleadingness into consideration while also being generalizable. In reality, however, categorizations of sources are made on a binary scale, neglecting more subtle forms of misinformation. Furthermore, this argument ignores that source ratings are sensitive to time and context. Sources may change, for instance, due to ownership. In addition, while source judgments can be made irrespective of topic expertise, such ratings are often based on journalistic norms of one media system that are not necessarily applicable to other contexts.

In conclusion, we see source-based approaches as a promising way to track misinformation online. However, for computational social scientists to study the spread of unreliable news, they require access to reliable and credible databases of source ratings. Since the 2016 U.S. elections, the most prominent way to track misinformation online has been to use a list of untrustworthy or unreliable domains \citep[e.g.,][]{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. The organization \href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/}{NewsGuard} offers the most comprehensive database, not just blacklisting domains but providing a trustworthiness score per domain. A team of journalists and editors rate the domains based on nine journalistic quality criteria and regularly updates both the coverage of the list, i.e., the number of domains, as well as their overall trustworthiness rating. Recently, the database has been extended to other countries than the United States and is constantly growing. With recent political events and crises, there has been a heightened demand for such databases, leading to an increase in NewsGuard's popularity. For instance, it has been used to study user's engagement with unreliable news in the context of the 2018 and 2020 US elections \citet{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a} or to assess if members of the Republican party in the U.S. Congress have contributed to the spread of misinformation by linking to untrustworthy sources \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a}. However, the database was created as a tool for brand security rather than research, which is why access is paywalled and expensive. In light of the discussed disadvantages of list-based approaches, we here provide a comprehensive description of the content of this database following the research question: How reliable is the NewsGuard database of domain trustworthiness ratings over time and language contexts? 

More precisely, we believe that it is essential to address the following aspects of the NewsGuard database in more detail: First, we look into the distribution and update process of the trustworthiness ratings as well as the selection and removal of sources over time. Second, we assess the completeness of the database for different countries, and lastly, we estimate the usefulness of other information provided in the database, e.g., journalistic criteria and political orientation. Therefore, we focus on three questions:

\textbf{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings, e.g., how often do ratings change or do domains vanish?} 
First, we investigate the development of the NewsGuard database over time. This is important to estimate whether using an older database will distort results. We describe the different versions of the database and reproduce published research relying on the NewsGuard list with different versions of the list to assess the degree to which results depend on a specific version of the list. 

\textbf{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?} A recent study has shown high agreement of NewsGuard with other expert-rated lists in the U.S. context~\citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}, but an assessment for other countries and contexts is missing. Therefore, we next compare the distribution and completeness of information provided by NewsGuard across different contexts. 

\textbf{How can other information be used for misinformation research?} For each source, NewsGuard experts rate journalistic quality criteria, topics, and political orientation, but they also give sources flags for issues. Estimating to what extent the different criteria constitute the overall trustworthiness score and the completeness of additional information might shed light on aspects of misinformation beyond binary true/false labels.

We also want to empower misinformation researchers to make informed decisions about whether the substantial expense associated with acquiring access to NewsGuard is necessary for their research. 

\section{Dataset construction}
NewsGuard collects the list of sources to rate from tracking online activity. On their website, NewsGuard claims to have reviewed domains accounting for 95\% of online engagement. The organization also manually adds sources they deem influential or that changed domains. The database automatically updates every hour and allows us to track changes going back to March 2019, when the organization started operating. Still, the procedure by which domains are selected or removed is undisclosed on the website, as are the intervals in which the trustworthiness assessments are updated in the database. 

As of December 15, 2023, the database covers 11,314 sources, out of which 9,946 are unique domains. Some sources, however, do not receive a trustworthiness score as they are classified as a ``platform'' (63 domains, e.g., YouTube). In addition, the database includes 63 sources judged as ``satire'' (e.g., The Onion) and 530 lifestyle sources (e.g., healthquotefree.com). NewsGuard does not rate these sources, but they make up 5.8\% of the listed sources in the database. For the other 10,658 ratings sources, the database includes the following types of ratings (see Table SX for a full list of variables):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unique identifier for each rating and the date of the last update
    \item Domain name and parent domain (e.g., nytimes.com)
    \item A trustworthiness rating as a continuous scale (between 0 and 100) and binary labels (N = Not Trustworthy/T = Trustworthy) based on thresholding the trustworthiness scale at 60
    \item Binary labels for nine journalistic quality criteria, each awarding a number of points which together constitute the overall trustworthiness rating
    \item Language and country associated with the domain
    \item Political orientation of the outlet
    \item Topics covered (e.g., ``local news'', ``political news'', ``health information'') by the outlet
    \item Flags indicating whether the outlet frequently posts certain issues or narratives.
\end{itemize}

\section{Descriptive Analyses}
\subsection{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings?}
A trained team of NewsGuard experts regularly judges the trustworthiness of the selected news sources based on nine journalistic criteria\footnote{\href{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/} that are treated globally for all sources. Therefore, the trustworthiness score is a composite score. In the following, we review how the database, and particularly the trustworthiness ratings and domain selection, have changed over the years.

\textbf{Coverage.} Over the span of four years, NewsGuard has grown from 2,615 to 10,658 sources with ratings, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}. NewsGuard regularly adds new sources to the database but rarely removes any, i.e., the database has been growing to three times its initial size. When comparing the added domains for one month to the previous one, NewsGuard has added 7,937 sources over time while they only removed 632. More precisely, the NewsGuard team adds new sources every month, with an average of 144 domains per month.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f1-sources_over_time.png}
    \caption{Number of unique domains in the NewsGuard database over time.}
    \label{fig:fig1}
\end{figure}

NewsGuard ratings are made accessible to customers in an online database (Amazon S3 bucket), where a current snapshot of the rating database is uploaded every hour. We sampled the snapshot database in monthly intervals, choosing a snapshot from the first hour of the 15th of every month. However, when we sampled from the second day of each month instead, we observed an irregular dip in the number of domains (see May 2022 in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}). We think an error caused this fluctuation, as the approximately 3,500 removed domains were added again two weeks later. This sudden disappearance and reappearance of domains illustrates the overall volatility of the database. We therefore advise researchers to inspect several database snapshots and check their consistency before choosing which one to use. 

Some domains appear multiple times; typically, these are sources that publish in different languages, i.e., they have the same domain but provide multi-lingual content, or they are also considered a global source. For instance, the Austrian website servustv.com produces both German- and English-language output. Therefore, it appears twice in the database: Once with the country label ``AT'' and once with ``ALL''. Except for three such cases, the majority of duplicated domains have identical trustworthiness ratings, though. Therefore, dropping duplicated domain entries does not substantially affect downstream research results. However, the multi-lingual domain coverage could be especially useful for comparative research. In these use cases, duplicated domains with different country labels should be kept.

\textbf{Distribution.} NewsGuard trustworthiness ratings are assigned on a scale from 0 to 100. Historically, NewsGuard considered sources with a rating below 60 as not trustworthy, therefore assigning binary trustworthiness labels\footnote{Historic snapshot of the description of rating process from December 31st, 2022 \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. Since February 2023\footnote{Historic snapshot of the changed rating page from February 1st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}, NewsGuard suggests a more granular categorization, classifying sources with a score of 100 as "high credibility", scores between 75 and 99 as "generally credible", scores between 60 and 74 as "credible with exceptions", scores between 40 and 59 as "proceed with caution", and scores between 0 and 39 as "proceed with maximum caution". Notably, NewsGuard is not a blacklist. In fact, close to 60\% of domains have an overall rating of over 60 (see Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2} for the distribution of trustworthiness scores). 
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f2-trustworthiness_distribution.png}
    \caption{Distribution of domain trustworthiness in the NewsGuard database on December 15, 2023.}
    \label{fig:fig2}
\end{figure}

When looking at the score distribution over time, the left skew diminishes slightly: while the average score was 71.8 (SD=33.3) in 2019, it is now at 64.5 (SD=32.4). In other words, the average trustworthiness of domains contained in the database has decreased over time. The distributions of trustworthiness from 2019 to 2023 are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}. Given that the database tripled in size and that added sources tend to have a lower trustworthiness score (on average 61.8, SD=27.7), this development might explain the decreasing trustworthiness scores over time. Especially in 2020, a great number of sources with lower ratings were added to the database. The highest number of sources added to the database was in September 2020, when 1,030 sources with low trustworthiness (M=35.3, SD=14.1) were added all at once. It is impossible to say whether this development is due to a sampling bias towards untrustworthy news or a global change in news trustworthiness. 

NewsGuard employs a different team of editors per country, potentially leading to country differences in coverage as well as ratings. The addition of countries to the database, however, has not directly affected the trustworthiness scores. Dips in trustworthiness, however, may be related to country-specific domains being added to the database. For instance, 1,015 of the 1,030 added sources in September 2020 are coded as U.S. Overall, smaller dips appear across all countries covered and are most likely related to many low-quality sources popping up around the time of the COVID-19 pandemic (see Figure~\ref{fig:figa1}). 

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f3-scores_over_time-violin.png}
    \caption{Distribution of trustworthiness scores per year}
    \label{fig:fig3}
\end{figure}

Another explanation for the decrease in trustworthiness scores could be updates of existing ratings. NewsGuard indicates the date when a rating was last updated, even if the score did not change. Therefore, each entry in the database has a unique identifier (``UUID''). Updates to entries happen on average once every nine months (279 days), but this varies strongly per domain, ranging from a few seconds to a maximum of 1,109 days between updates (but with an interquartile range from 186 to 388 days). When updated, trustworthiness scores mostly remain untouched, i.e., the majority of sources have not decreased in trustworthiness. However, a few domains drastically dropped in trustworthiness; for instance, conservativedailynews.com lost 80 points in trustworthiness in one update in November 2020 (see Figure~\ref{fig:figa2} that illustrates the differences in consecutive scores over time, with major score updates in early 2020). Overall, the decrease in trustworthiness that we can see in Figure~\ref{fig:fig3} can, therefore, likely be attributed to NewsGuard adding untrustworthy sources rather than previously trustworthy domains losing their reliability. 

\textbf{Composition of trustworthiness score.} 
The trustworthiness score is a composite score that is calculated based on nine journalistic criteria. For each domain, the organization assigns a Boolean value (yes/no) per criterion. The trustworthiness score is then calculated as the sum of the weighted criteria. Because of the discrete point contributions to the overall score, the resulting trustworthiness score has a limited number of values it can take based on the different combinations of categories. The criteria are weighted differently, ordered in descending relevance:

\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'', changed to ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' in December 2023 (22 points)
  \item ``Gathers and presents information responsibly'' (18 points) 
  \item ``Regularly corrects or clarifies errors'', changed to ``Has effective practices for correcting errors''(12.5 points)
  \item ``Handles the difference between news and opinion responsibly'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' (10 points)
  \item ``Website discloses ownership and financing' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Clearly labels advertising'' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Reveals who's in charge, including possible conflicts of interest'' (5 points)
  \item ``The site provides the names of content creators, along with either contact or biographical information'' (5 points)
\end{itemize}

Further explanation of the criteria and their weights can be found in the NewsGuard FAQ\footnote{Here taken from December 31st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. 

In the latest version of the dataset, the criterion ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' was the most respected, and 8,903 of the sources satisfied it (83.6\% of sources), followed by ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' (82\% of sources). When one of the two criteria is satisfied, the other one tends to as well (Pearson's r = 0.88). A factor analysis with oblique rotation confirms that the two criteria make up one factor (accounting for 56.4\% of variance explained and with factor loadings > 0.9), while all other criteria share 36.5\% of the variance explained (factor loadings >.6). Meeting one of two criteria strongly predicts a high rating, and vice versa (with coefficients of 61.91, p <.001, and 61.94, p < .001, respectively). In line with that, 13.5\% of the ratings only meet these two criteria, while 10.2\% of the sources satisfy none of the criteria. The most common combination of criteria, however, is that all criteria are satisfied (15.7\% of ratings)––given that the majority of sources are considered trustworthy. A correlation matrix of criteria can be found in the Appendix (~\ref{table:tablea1}.

Furthermore, looking at domain criteria updates over time, we find that most sources also keep their scores. The most common update pattern is a negative change in the criterion ``Website discloses ownership and financing'', occurring, for example, once for the German weekly newspaper zeit.de. This criterion appears to be the most volatile, with 12\% of sources eventually changing their label negatively. On the other hand, a positive update is also the third most common pattern (with 8.6\% of sources), indicating that criteria related to ownership might be a criterion that is frequently updated/monitored by NewsGuard. Update patterns of singular criteria over time are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:fig4}.

\subsection{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?}
\textbf{Country coverage.} The dataset (2019-2023) contains sources from nine countries in total (United States, Great Britain, Italy, Canada, France, Germany, Austria, Australia, and New Zealand) plus sources considered to be global. Countries were added in the following order: GB, US, ALL, IT, FR, DE (all in 2019), CA (2021), AU, AT (2022), and NZ (2023). 

The majority of sources are from the United States (76\%), followed by global sources (13.8\%). Table~\ref{table:table1} gives an overview of the number of sources per country and their average trustworthiness scores. NewsGuard also clusters sources based on language, with English being the most represented (10,520 sources; 88.3\%), followed by Italian (4.4\%), French (3.9\%), German (3.4\%), and one Spanish source (0.01\%). 

\input{tables/t1_countries.tex}

Across all versions of the database and most countries covered by NewsGuard, the overall trustworthiness of sources remains >60, except for the United States: U.S.-based outlets are of lower quality on average (see Table~\ref{table:table1}). Figure~\ref{fig:fig5} shows the average trustworthiness of sources per country over time. It is unclear whether U.S. sources are of lower trustworthiness overall--another intuitive explanation for this finding could also be related to the relative number of outlets in the dataset. 

Due to the size of the database per country, the U.S. database was updated on average 5,396 updates per year (32,367 times in total, following our sampling strategy), while the maximum number of times that the second most comprehensive database, Italy, was updated is only 2,021. Times between updates of sources vary substantially, ranging from 235 days (New Zealand), 303 days (U.S.) to 403 days (Germany). Updating patterns do not seem to depend on the country. Rather, domains get updated once a year per country. Overall, however, the U.S. coverage seems the most complete and reliable, given the total number of outlets rated by NewsGuard.

(2024) collected a list of German online news domains (GOND)\footnote{\href{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}}, which shares 212 German and 505 global domains with NewsGuard.

\subsection{How can other information be used for misinformation research?}
\textbf{Political Orientation.} The dataset also classifies sources according to their political orientation. The classification relies on a left-to-right conception of the political spectrum. In December 2022, the labels shifted from four (far left/ slightly left/slightly right/far right) to only two possible labels (left/right). 

Overall, the political orientation dimension covers only a minority of the sources in the database. In the current version of the database, only 31.9\% of the sources have a rating for political orientation (2,980 sources). The ratio decreases when zooming in on singular countries: 38.73\% of news outlets have a political orientation rating in the United States, whereas only 10.08\% have such a rating in Great Britain, 14.23\% in Italy, 17.6\% in Germany, and 22.63\% in France. Here, again, the over-representation of the United States in the data set becomes apparent. Table~\ref{table:table2} shows the ratings per country and the percentages of coverage per country. In most countries, the majority of sources with political orientation ratings are judged as belonging to the right-wing spectrum. 

\input{tables/t2_orientation_countries.tex}

Trustworthiness ratings are generally lower for right-leaning sources. In the current database (December 2023), the mean trustworthiness score for right-leaning sources is 26.39 (SD=19.93), while it is 66.28 (SD=22.45) for left-leaning sources (t(3394)=35.35, p < .001), Mdiff=44.66, t(17852)=100.26, p < .001. Before the rating system changed, left- and right-leaning sources had moderate trustworthiness ratings (for example, in June 2021, 82.16 and 61.9, respectively) in contrast to the far left and far right sources. In August/September 2021, however, when a high number of untrustworthy sources were added to the database, also the trustworthiness of right-leaning sources dropped by almost 23 points, see Figure~\ref{fig:fig6}. When the ratings changed to left/right, this classification also to a lower average score of right-leaning sources by adding low-quality sources from what was classified as "far right" before (where trustworthiness ranged between 13.48 and 17.84 points).

\textbf{Topics.} The database assigns topic labels, which are based on the topics covered by the outlets. The topics are not mutually exclusive, i.e., a source can be assigned to multiple topics. The most popular topics in the database are ``Political News or Commentary'' (37.89\% of ratings), ``Conspiracy Theories or Hoaxes'' (17.93\%), and ``Health or medical information'' (17.24\%). The topic ratings are also not complete: Out of all ratings, 18,245 ratings (40.9\%) are not classified under any topic, but in the most recent version of the database, only 282 domains are without a topic (2.9\%). 

Up until 2022, fewer topic labels were given (see Fig.~\ref{fig:figa3}), but throughout the covered time frame, the most popular topics remained the same. Especially health-related information, as well as political news, peaks at least once a year, most likely coinciding with update intervals. Interestingly, only about a year after the COVID-19 pandemic started, in August 2021, 1,468 labels related to COVID-19 misinformation were added to the database. This update coincides with a drop in trustworthiness for right-wing sources, as described above.

Trustworthiness ratings greatly differ across topics. The quality of sources linked to each topic is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fig8}. Sources covering conspiracy theories have the lowest average rating (M=10.62, SD=11.05), while those covering ``Education'' (M=92.18, SD=8.18) and ``Local News'' (M=87.95, SD=12.69) have the highest average trustworthiness scores.

Across time, the most popular category, ``Political News and Commentary,'' attracts the highest share of bad sources: 79.85\% of all untrustworthy sources are said to cover that topic, making up 48.64\% of all unique sources having covered that topic. The ``COVID-19 misinformation'' label contains 51.60\% of all untrustworthy domains, while the ``Conspiracy'' label contains only 38.14\% of the untrustworthy sources. COVID-related misinformation or conspiracy seems to only make up a small proportion of all untrustworthy information, while most of it relates to politicized information. Nonetheless, the following topics are typical topics for untrustworthy information: ``Conspiracy theories and hoaxes'' (99\% of sources covering this topic are untrustworthy), ``COVID-19 misinformation'' (96.96\%), ``Political news or commentary'' (78.28\%), ``Health or medical information'' (75.62\%), ``Viral content'' (48.62\%). 

Topic trustworthiness heavily depends on the context, and variance within topics largely depends on the country and the political orientation of the source. For instance, for medical information, sources in Canada are rated as relatively trustworthy (M=64, SD=34.55). In the U.S., the average is below 60 (M=20.44, SD=28.98). In Figure~\ref{fig:fig9}, we illustrate the trustworthiness averages per country for the five least trustworthy topics. Especially for health and political news, we observe large differences between countries. For instance, Great Britain overall shows high trustworthiness for both topics (>60), while the U.S. and Italy have scores below 60.

\section{Conclusion}
Main findings:
Overall, more than half of the rated news is considered trustworthy. 
The skewed distribution of scores reflects the presence of misinformation on the right-wing spectrum. Does that mean that there are more news outlets on the right spectrum, or does NewsGuard have a bias?
The database gets updated at least once a year.


Implications:
Using a monthly snapshot of the database should be enough to cover changes
Always consider the country context: English-speaking countries (U.S. and U.K.) coverage is great; Italy, France, and Germany depend.

Limitations:
Misses user-created misinformation
