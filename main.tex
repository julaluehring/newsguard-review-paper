\begin{abstract}
\noindent To study the spread of misinformation on digital platforms, it is imperative for computational social scientists to have access to reliable and valid tools for identifying misinformation. A prominent way to track misinformation is using a list of web domains of news sources. The organization NewsGuard offers the most comprehensive database of source trustworthiness ratings and is increasing in popularity despite restrictive access. Yet, the database was neither created as a research instrument nor has it ever been reviewed systematically for this purpose. Therefore, we provide a comprehensive description and inspection of the NewsGuard database across the variability of trustworthiness ratings over time, the selection of sources, and the completeness of news categories, such as political orientation. By describing the database over time and across countries, we outline potential pitfalls and recommendations for digital media research and discuss appropriate use cases. 
\end{abstract}
\keywords{\textit{misinformation, news trustworthiness, NewsGuard} \vspace{8ex}}

\section{Introduction}
Misinformation permeates both social media platforms \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018} and mainstream media channels \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. 
However, only a fraction of news consumption is attributed to false news -- roughly ~0.15\% \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020} -- and only a small group of users actively engage with unreliable information online \citep{grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019}. 
Typically, these individuals hold strong political or ideological beliefs and accept and actively look for information that echoes their opinions \citep{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022} -- information that often comes from political elites or established media sources \citep{tsfatiCausesConsequencesMainstream2020}. 
Consequently, it is unsurprising that misinformation capitalizes on these dynamics to stoke conflict and evoke negative emotions, thereby exacerbating partisan divides and fueling inter-group hostility \citep{gonzalez-bailonAsymmetricIdeologicalSegregation2023a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. 
Against a backdrop of declining trust in both media outlets \citep{newmanReutersInstituteDigital2023} and institutions \citep{bennettDisinformationAge2020}, it becomes increasingly crucial to comprehend the mechanisms driving the dissemination of misinformation, particularly in the digital sphere. 
Digital media not only facilitate the measurement of these dynamics but also have the potential to reinforce polarization and misinformation \citep{lorenz-spreenSystematicReviewWorldwide2022}.

The spread of misinformation has been studied extensively by scholars from various scientific disciplines, relying on different conceptualizations and terms \cite{guessMisinformationDisinformationOnline2020}.
%what do they have in common and how do they differ? 
%definition influences measurement, measurement influences definition!
In psychology, researchers commonly use misinformation as an umbrella term for inaccurate or unreliable information, regardless of intent, e.g., \cite{eckerPsychologicalDriversMisinformation2022} or \cite{vanderlindenMisinformationSusceptibilitySpread2022}. 
In communication science, a popular definition by Claire Wardle and Hossein Derakhshan (\citeyear{wardleInformationDisorderInterdisciplinary2017}) distinguishes non-intentional misinformation from disinformation designed with the intent to misinform, including fake news that pursues financial or political intentions and conspiracy theories, summarizing them under the umbrella term information disorder. According to this definition, misinformation covers only false and misleading information that is created unintentionally, such as honest mistakes or incomplete reporting.
Computational social scientists, such as David Lazer and colleagues (\citeyear{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}), tend to follow this notion of information disorders:
"Fake news overlaps with other information disorders, such as misinformation (false or misleading information) and disinformation (false information that is purposely spread to deceive people)." (p. 1094) 

The ambiguous nature of misinformation, and the ethical challenge of who defines misinformation, presents researchers with numerous challenges, leaving them with a number of degrees of freedom. 
Defining the boundaries of misinformation, essentially deciding where to draw the line to the truth, is a very complex task for numerous reasons: 
First, misinformation often appears in subtle forms rather than obvious lies \citep{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a, bakirFakeNewsEconomy2018a, vargoAgendasettingPowerFake2018}, making it hard to define where misinformation starts. In the real world, professional editors and politicians leave out true but important details, or they decontextualize parts to fit a narrative or a storyline without saying anything wrong. Implicit interpretations of facts can be different from explicit lying, making subtle forms of misinformation hard to detect, especially using automated methods (REF Noelle).
Second, making this decision involves a number of ethical issues, and different groups of people would come to different conclusions. Trying to consider these challenges, researchers have often retreated to investigating binary (false/true) examples of misinformation, here nobody would argue against the examples of misinformation––solving the ethical problem. 
Third, conceptual choices are often shaped by methodological constraints (e.g., the type of data that is available) or disciplinary preferences (e.g., the methodological traditions), which is why misinformation is often used as a general umbrella term for different phenomena \citep{weeksWhatNextSix2021}.
Consequently, this semantic variance has contributed to imprecise or inconclusive findings regarding the prevalence and impact of misinformation. 
While some scholars, such as \citet{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a}, argue that the effects of misinformation are overstated, calling it a symptom of broader social issues, others, like \cite{tayThinkingClearlyMisinformation2024}, reason that the heterogeneity in measuring misinformation contributes to divergent evidence and creates a false dichotomy between understanding misinformation as a symptom or a cause.

To study the diffusion or discussion of misinformation on social media, computational researchers commonly opt for either story- or source-based methodologies. Both approaches rely on experts, such as fact-checkers, to curate a list of stories or sources, which then serves as the foundation for collecting data from digital platforms. These approaches, however, operate at different levels of analysis, and the choice between them depends on the primary research objective. When the goal is to study specific instances of misinformation with high certainty, e.g., focusing on a specific case, the story-based approach proves advantageous. By selecting a handful of (fact-checked) stories, researchers can ensure low variance in accuracy ratings and achieve high precision in capturing misinformation. For instance, Vosoughi et al. \citeyear{vosoughiSpreadTrueFalse2018a} traced the diffusion of rumors on Twitter (now X) using links to fact-checked stories, while Charquero-Ballester et al. \citeyear{charquero-ballesterDifferentTypesCOVID192021} tried to scale up URL-searches by classifying tweets mentioning keywords related to fact-checked false stories. Additionally, this approach frees researchers of the responsibility to judge the trustworthiness of individual stories, as experts have already verified factual components of the stories, and identified components of the story that are false.

However, relying solely on a small selection of fact-checked stories has its limitations. Singular stories, while clearly false and widely debated, represent only a fraction of misinformation present on social media platforms, and it is impossible to generalize findings beyond that specific, potentially biased case. Moreover, these forms of operationalization may not encapsulate the full spectrum of misinformation and largely neglect more subtle (likely not fact-checked) and harder-to-detect forms of misinformation that have been shown to be more persistent \citep{altayMisinformationMisinformationConceptual2023a, hameleersDegreesDeceptionEffects2021a}. Furthermore, the selection bias inherent in fact-checking organizations' coverage may skew the representation of misinformation, neglecting topics for which they lack expertise at a given time. Consequently, this approach tends to underestimate both the prevalence and impact of misinformation, failing to capture and judge the harmfulness of its broader dynamics effectively. 

Source-based strategies offer the advantage of encompassing a broader spectrum of sources and narratives, thus enriching the sample by considering a more diverse information environment. Source-based approaches also enable the inclusion of subtler forms of misinformation and facilitate accurate estimations of both the scale \citep{allenEvaluatingFakeNews2020, grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019,guessExposureUntrustworthyWebsites2020a, yangCOVID19InfodemicTwitter2021a} and dynamics of dissemination \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a, pennycookShiftingAttentionAccuracy2021a, robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a, shaoSpreadLowcredibilityContent2018}. Nevertheless, it's important to note that not every story originating from an untrustworthy source is necessarily false, introducing a degree of inaccuracy at the level of individual articles. Despite potential accuracy errors at this granularity, source-based judgments of trustworthiness can be accurate at the macroscopic level. For example, the same source may publish accurate reports of events, but they may also publish inaccurate opinion pieces. Hence, variance in article accuracy can be covered by a lower trustworthiness score at the level of the source. Another example is that unreliable publishers who predominantly share unreliable news articles may occasionally publish reliable news pieces to attract new (or keep existing) audiences.

The precision of source-based approaches hinges largely on the selection of sources within a given list. Strictly speaking, this conceptualization covers information disseminated by unreliable sources -- making it essential to recognize that observed patterns may be intricately linked to the inherent biases or external forces on the selection of sources. For instance, an unbalanced selection of unreliable sources may reveal patterns due to that imbalance rather than patterns unique to misinformation. This underscores the importance of also assessing factors such as political biases or editorial practices of the sources, considering that a lack of editorial oversight can compromise the quality of news reporting \citep{lazerScienceFakeNews2018}. In other words, to obtain a balanced selection of sources and account for the influence of source-related variables other than reliability, source-based approaches can incorporate assessments of political, cultural, or editorial biases and misleading tendencies while maintaining generalizability. Nonetheless, a number of problems remain in practice: First, source categorizations often oversimplify the nuanced spectrum of misinformation, often relying on a binary classification system (fake/real). Second, it is crucial to acknowledge that source ratings vary across time and cultural contexts, for example, following a change in ownership or editorial practices. Third, while source judgments are typically made without requiring specific topic expertise, they often reflect the level of journalistic professionalism and editorial norms of a particular media ecosystem, which may not be universally applicable across different contexts \citep{bruggemannHallinManciniRevisited2014b}.

Taken together, source-based approaches are a promising way to track misinformation online. However, to study the spread of unreliable news, computational social scientists need access to reliable and credible databases of source ratings. Since the 2016 U.S. elections, the most popular way to track misinformation online has been to use a list of untrustworthy or unreliable sources and search for their web domains in URL text \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a, grinbergFakeNewsTwitter2019, guessExposureUntrustworthyWebsites2020a, shaoSpreadLowcredibilityContent2018, yangCOVID19InfodemicTwitter2021a}. The organization \href{https://www.newsguardtech.com/}{NewsGuard} offers the most comprehensive list. It does not just blacklist untrustworthy sources but provides a continuous trustworthiness score for each source, covering news sources across the entire spectrum of news quality. A team of journalists and editors rates the news sources based on nine journalistic quality criteria and regularly updates both the coverage of the list (increasing the number of sources) and their overall trustworthiness rating. The database is constantly growing and has recently been extended to countries beyond the United States. With recent political events and crises, demand for such databases has increased, enhancing the popularity of NewsGuard. For instance, it has been used to study user engagement with unreliable news during the 2016 and 2020 US elections \citet{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a} or to assess if Republican US Congress members have contributed to spreading misinformation by sharing links to untrustworthy sources \citep{lasserSocialMediaSharing2022a}. 

Importantly, however, the NewsGuard database was initially created as a tool for brand security rather than scientific research, which is also why access is paywalled and expensive. In light of the mentioned disadvantages of source-based approaches, we provide a comprehensive description of the content of this database. We aim to answer the following main research question: How reliable is the NewsGuard database of source trustworthiness ratings over time and language contexts? For this, we first look into how trustworthiness ratings are distributed, the process of updating them, and the selection and removal of sources over time. Second, we assess the completeness of the database for different countries. Lastly, we estimate the usefulness of other information provided in the database, such as journalistic criteria and the political orientation of sources. In order to assess different aspects of the database, we introduce three sub-questions:

\textbf{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings, e.g., how often do ratings change or do sources vanish?} 
Investigating the development of the NewsGuard database over time is important to estimate whether using an older database will distort results. We describe the different versions of the database and reproduce published research relying on the NewsGuard list with different versions of the list to assess the degree to which results depend on a specific version of the list. 

\textbf{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?} A recent study has shown high agreement of NewsGuard with other expert-rated lists in the U.S. context~\citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023}, but an assessment for other countries is missing. Therefore, we next compare the distribution and completeness of information provided by NewsGuard across different contexts. 

\textbf{How can other source labels in the NewsGuard database be used for misinformation research?} NewsGuard experts categorize each source on nine binary journalistic quality criteria. The criteria are weighted and aggregated to a continuous trustworthiness score. In addition, NewsGuard labels the topics covered as well as the political orientation of the source. %Analyzing the importance of journalistic criteria to trustworthiness ratings as well as the completeness of additional information might shed light on aspects of misinformation beyond binary true/false labels used in other lists.

By describing the database, we outline potential pitfalls for digital media research and offer recommendations for the application of the NewsGuard database in different contexts. We also want to empower misinformation researchers to make informed decisions about whether the substantial expense associated with acquiring access to NewsGuard is necessary for their research. 

\section{Dataset construction}
NewsGuard collects the list of sources by tracking online activity. On their website, NewsGuard claims to have reviewed news sources accounting for 95\% of online engagement. The organization also manually adds sources they deem influential, or that changed their web domain. The database automatically updates every hour and allows tracking changes back to March 2019, when the organization started operating. Still, the procedure by which domains are selected or removed is undisclosed on the website, as are the intervals at which the trustworthiness assessments are updated. 

As of December 15, 2023, the database has 11,314 entries in total, some without a trustworthiness rating. This includes 63 sources classified as a ``platform'' (e.g., YouTube), 63 sources judged as ``satire'' (e.g., The Onion) and 530 lifestyle sources (e.g., healthquotefree.com). NewsGuard does not rate these sources, but they make up 5.8\% of the listed sources in the database. For the remaining 10,658 ratings sources, the database includes the following characteristics (or variables) (see Table~\ref{table:tablea1} for a full list of variables):
\begin{itemize}
    \item Unique identifier for each rating and date of the last update
    \item Domain name and parent domain (e.g., nytimes.com)
    \item A trustworthiness rating as a continuous scale (between 0 and 100) and binary labels (N = Not Trustworthy/T = Trustworthy) based on a threshold at 60
    \item Language and country associated with the source
    \item Political orientation of the source
    \item Topics covered (e.g., ``local news'', ``political news'', ``health information'') by the source
\end{itemize}

A trained team of NewsGuard experts regularly judges the trustworthiness of the selected news sources based on nine journalistic criteria\footnote{\href{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20240131155450/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}} that are treated globally for all sources across countries. The trustworthiness score is a composite score of these nine indicators. Binary labels for nine journalistic quality criteria, each awarding a number of points which together constitute the overall trustworthiness rating. The criteria are weighted differently, ordered in descending relevance:

\begin{itemize}
  \item ``Does not repeatedly publish false content'', changed to ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' in December 2023 (22 points)
  \item ``Gathers and presents information responsibly'' (18 points) 
  \item ``Regularly corrects or clarifies errors'', changed to ``Has effective practices for correcting errors''(12.5 points)
  \item ``Handles the difference between news and opinion responsibly'' (12.5 points)
  \item ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' (10 points)
  \item ``Website discloses ownership and financing' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Clearly labels advertising'' (7.5 points)
  \item ``Reveals who's in charge, including possible conflicts of interest'' (5 points)
  \item ``The site provides the names of content creators, along with either contact or biographical information'' (5 points)
\end{itemize}

\section{Descriptive Analyses}
\subsection{How volatile are trustworthiness ratings?}
In the following, we review how the database, and particularly the trustworthiness ratings and source selection, have changed over the years.

\textbf{Coverage across time.} Over the span of four years, NewsGuard has grown from 2,615 to 10,658 sources with ratings, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}. NewsGuard regularly adds new sources but rarely removes any, i.e., the database has been growing to three times its initial size. When counting the added domains based on monthly comparisons of the list, NewsGuard has added 7,937 domains over time in total but only removed 632. More precisely, the NewsGuard team adds an average of 144 new domains per month.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f1-sources_over_time.png}
    \caption{Number of unique domains in the NewsGuard database over time.}
    \label{fig:fig1}
\end{figure}

NewsGuard ratings are available for customers in an online database (Amazon S3 bucket), where a current snapshot is uploaded every hour. We sampled a snapshot from the first hour of the 15th of every month. When we sampled from the second day of each month as a test, we observed an irregular dip in the number of sources (see May 2022 in Figure~\ref{fig:fig1}). Probably, an error caused this fluctuation, as the approximately 3,500 removed sources were added again two weeks later. Yet, this sudden disappearance and reappearance of sources illustrate the overall volatility of the database. We therefore advise researchers to inspect several database snapshots and check their consistency before choosing one. 

The most recent snapshot contains ratings for only 9,946 unique domains (compared to 11,314 entries in total); in other words, some sources appear multiple times. Typically, these are sources that publish in different languages, i.e., they have the same web domain but provide multi-lingual content, or they have an entry as a local and a global source. For instance, the Austrian website servustv.com produces both German- and English-language output and appears with the country label ``AT'' and the global label ``ALL''. Except in three cases, the majority of duplicated domains have identical trustworthiness ratings. Therefore, dropping duplicated domain entries does not substantially affect downstream research results. However, the multi-lingual source coverage could be especially useful for comparative research. For this use case, researchers should keep duplicated domains.

Furthermore, 5,186 of the rated domains belong to only 403 unique parent domains. Some parent domains have multiple variations of the domain name, probably representing the same source, e.g., theragingpatriot.org has domains ending with .blog, .com, .net, .com, .pro, and .net. Other parent domains have very different domains, e.g., 24usnews.com and africdaily.com belong to charmdaily.com. The other 6,128 ratings do not have a parent domain entry in the database. NewsGuard often updates parent domains instead of each domain, and they assign the same unique identifier to those domains. Therefore, filtering updates by the unique identifier can be misleading.  

\textbf{Distribution.} NewsGuard trustworthiness ratings range from 0 to 100. Historically, NewsGuard considered sources with a rating below 60 as not trustworthy, therefore assigning binary trustworthiness labels\footnote{Snapshot of the described rating process from December 31st, 2022 \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20221231134910/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. Since February 2023\footnote{Snapshot of the updated rating process from February 1st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20230201003836/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}, NewsGuard uses a more granular categorization, classifying sources with a score of 100 as "high credibility", scores between 75 and 99 as "generally credible", scores between 60 and 74 as "credible with exceptions", scores between 40 and 59 as "proceed with caution", and scores between 0 and 39 as "proceed with maximum caution". Notably, NewsGuard is not a blacklist. In fact, close to 60\% of sources have an overall rating of over 60 (see Fig.~\ref{fig:fig2} for the distribution of trustworthiness scores). 

When looking at the score distribution over time, the left skew diminishes slightly: while the average score was 71.8 (SD=33.3) in 2019, it is now at 64.5 (SD=32.4). The distributions of trustworthiness from 2019 to 2023 are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2}. Various explanations for decreases in trustworthiness over time are possible. First, the database has tripled in size and added sources having a lower trustworthiness score (on average 61.8, SD=27.7). Especially during the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, a great number of sources with lower ratings were added. The highest number of sources added was in September 2020, with 1,030 sources with low trustworthiness (M=35.3, SD=14.1). It is not clear if this development is due to a sampling effort to include more untrustworthy news or a global increase in untrustworthy sources. 

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f2-panel.png}
    \caption{Distribution of trustworthiness A) in the first and most recent database and B) per year (with M and SD), standardized by count}
    \label{fig:fig2}
\end{figure}

Second, NewsGuard employs different teams of editors per country, potentially leading to country differences in coverage and ratings. Dips in trustworthiness may be related to country-specific sources being added (e.g., 1,015 of the 1,030 added sources in September 2020 are coded as U.S., see Figure~\ref{figa1} for the number of sources added per country over time). In contrast, adding entirely new countries seems to have little effect on the trustworthiness scores (see Figure~\ref{fig:fig3}). Overall, smaller dips appear across all countries covered and are most likely related to many low-quality sources popping up around the time of the COVID-19 pandemic. 

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/f3-scores_by_country.png}
    \caption{Changes in trustworthiness scores over time, with colors indicating the countries added to the database and size representing the number of sources added.}
    \label{fig:fig3}
\end{figure}

A third possibility is updates of existing ratings. NewsGuard indicates the date when a rating was last updated, even if the score did not change. Updates to entries happen on average once every eight months (243 days), but this varies strongly per source between a few seconds to a maximum of 1,109 days (interquartile range: 162 to 355 days). When updated, trustworthiness scores mostly remain unchanged, i.e., the majority of sources have not decreased in trustworthiness. However, a few sources drastically dropped in trustworthiness; for instance, conservativedailynews.com lost 80 points in one update in November 2020. Figure~\ref{fig:figa2} illustrates the differences in consecutive scores over time, with major score updates in early 2020. Overall, the decrease in trustworthiness that we can see in the right-hand plot in Figure~\ref{fig:fig2} can, therefore, likely be attributed to NewsGuard adding untrustworthy sources rather than previously trustworthy sources losing their reliability. 

\textbf{Composition of trustworthiness score.} 
The trustworthiness score is a composite score that is calculated based on nine journalistic criteria. For each source, the organization assigns a Boolean value (yes/no) per criterion. The trustworthiness score is then calculated as the sum of the weighted criteria. Because of the discrete point contributions to the overall score, the resulting trustworthiness score has a limited number of values it can take based on the different combinations of categories. Further explanation of the criteria and their weights can be found in the NewsGuard FAQ\footnote{Here taken from December 31st, 2023: \href{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}{https://web.archive.org/web/20231230155214/https://www.newsguardtech.com/ratings/rating-process-criteria/}}. 

In the latest version of the dataset, 83.6\% of sources fulfill the criterion ``Avoids deceptive headlines'' alone (8,903 sources). It is followed by ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'', which is met by 82\% of all sources. These two top criteria often go together: When one of them is satisfied, the other one tends to be as well (Pearson's correlation coefficient = 0.88). 
A factor analysis with oblique rotation confirms that the two criteria load on one factor, accounting for 56.4\% of the explained variance, with factor loadings > 0.9. All other criteria share 36.5\% of the variance explained (factor loadings >.6). 
%Meeting any of the two criteria strongly predicts a high rating (with regression coefficients of \(\beta\)=61.91, p<.001, and \(\beta\)=61.94, p<.001, respectively). 
In line with that, 13.5\% of all sources meet both of the top criteria combined, while 10.2\% of sources satisfy none of the nine journalistic criteria. The most common combination of criteria is that all nine criteria are satisfied at the same time (15.7\% of ratings)––given that the majority of sources are considered trustworthy. A correlation matrix of criteria can be found in the Appendix (Table~\ref{table:tablea1}.

Looking at the journalistic criteria values over time, we find that most sources also keep their scores during updates. The most common change is a negative one (i.e., lowering trustworthiness) in the criterion ``Website discloses ownership and financing''. It occurs, for example, once for the German weekly newspaper zeit.de. This criterion appears to be the most volatile, with 12\% of sources eventually changing their label negatively. On the other hand, a positive change (i.e., increasing trustworthiness) for this criterion is the third most common pattern (with 8.6\% of sources), indicating that criteria related to ownership might be frequently monitored by NewsGuard. Update patterns of singular criteria over time are presented in Figure~\ref{fig:fig4}.

\subsection{How reliable and complete is the provided information, particularly for non-US countries?}
\textbf{Country coverage.} The dataset (2019-2023) contains sources from nine countries in total (United States, Great Britain, Italy, Canada, France, Germany, Austria, Australia, and New Zealand) plus sources considered to be global. Countries were added in the following order: GB, US, ALL, IT, FR, DE (all in 2019), CA (2021), AU, AT (2022), and NZ (2023). 

The majority of sources are from the United States (76\%), followed by global sources (13.8\%). Table~\ref{table:table2} gives an overview of the total number of unique sources per country and their average trustworthiness scores. NewsGuard also clusters sources based on language, with English being the most represented (8,417 sources; 88.1\%), followed by Italian (4.8\%), French (3.7\%), and German (3.5\%).

\input{tables/t2_countries.tex}

Across all versions of the database and most countries covered by NewsGuard, the overall trustworthiness of sources remains >65, except for the United States: U.S.-based outlets are of lower quality on average (see Table~\ref{table:table2}). Figure~\ref{fig:fig5} shows the average trustworthiness of sources per country over time (also see Figure~\ref{fig:figa3} and Figure~\ref{fig:figa4} for trustworthiness distribution per country). The development of trustworthiness indicates that for countries with good coverage (a higher number of sources), the database seems to have settled into a stable state, whereas for newer countries (CA, NZ, AU), rating coverage seems to vary. It is unclear whether U.S. sources are of lower trustworthiness overall or if the relative number of outlets in the dataset can explain this finding.

When comparing the number of sources that meet the journalistic criteria per country, we observe some striking patterns. Table~\ref{table:table3} shows the percentage of sources that miss a given indicator per country, i.e., a high percentage indicates that many sources fail to meet the criterion. For most countries, more than 50\% of sources meet each criterion. Interestingly, almost 80\% of Italian sources do not provide names of content creators, and over 70\% do not effectively correct errors. A similar but less pronounced pattern is visible for French sources. Across all criteria, the United States shows the highest percentage of sources not meeting the criteria. This indicates that lower trustworthiness scores in Figure~\ref{fig:fig5} and Table~\ref{table:table2} result from a high number of sources not meeting the journalistic quality criteria, as well as a high number of sources not meeting the two criteria with the biggest weights (18.8\% for ``Does not repeatedly publish false or egregiously misleading content'' and 47.5\% for ``Gathers and presents information responsibly'').

Due to the size of the database per country, the U.S. database was updated on average 6,306 times per year (37,839 times in total, based on our sampling strategy). The maximum number of times that Italy, the second largest database, was updated is only 663. Intervals between updates of sources vary substantially (see Table~\ref{table:table2}, ranging from 235 days (New Zealand), 262 days (U.S.) to 399 days (Germany). Updating patterns seem to depend on the country, with sources getting updated once a year per country on average. Overall, however, the U.S. coverage seems the most complete and reliable, given the total number of outlets rated by NewsGuard.

\textbf{Comparison with other lists.} In order to measure the actual coverage of sources per country, it is necessary to compare the sources covered by NewsGuard with other lists. However, other lists are often not compiled with the same objective as Newsguard, which is to cover all sources responsible for most of the traffic. Therefore, their scope and rating system may differ. In addition, other lists are not available for every country. 

For the U.S. context, ~\citet{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023} have compared five lists of existing expert ratings with NewsGuard and found a high number of non-overlapping web domains, although the quality of ratings largely overlapped. Further, Puschmann et al. (2024) collected a list of German online news domains (GOND)\footnote{\href{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}{https://osf.io/s5uhb/}}, largely relying on web tracking data of a representative sample of 1,500 German citizens. In the first version of the list, the authors also classified the domains into types, among others ``legacy press'' (49\%), ``digital-born news outlet'' (18.0\%), or ``hyperpartisan news'' (8.2\%). GOND shares 543 global sources with NewsGuard, with 147 English-speaking, 47 French, and 56 Italian sources. Out of all global sources that overlap, 285 are legacy press, and 56 are hyperpartisan news. Furthermore, GOND covers the same 237 German sources as NewsGuard, including 24 hyperpartisan and 19 digital-born news outlets. Overall, it seems that NewsGuard's coverage of the United States and Germany is extensive, while comparisons for other countries are missing.  
 
\subsection{How can other information be used for misinformation research?}
\textbf{Political Orientation.} The dataset also classifies sources according to their political orientation. The classification relies on a left-to-right conception of the political spectrum. In December 2022, the labels shifted from four (far left/ slightly left/slightly right/far right) to only two possible labels (left/right). 

Overall, the political orientation label is only available for a minority of the sources. In the current version of the database, 31.2\% of the sources have a label for political orientation (2,980 sources). The percentage decreases for most individual countries: 41.1\% of news outlets have a political orientation label in the United States, but only 9.8\% in Great Britain, 11.6\% in Italy, 16.1\% in Germany, and 15.3\% in France. Here, again, the over-representation of the United States in the data set becomes apparent. Table~\ref{table:table4} shows the total number of political orientation labels per country as well as the number and percentage of sources classified as left or right-leaning in the most recent database.
In most countries, the majority of sources with political orientation labels belong to the right-wing spectrum. Figure~\ref{fig:figa5} shows the distribution of political orientation per country over time.

Trustworthiness ratings are generally lower for right-leaning sources. In the current database (December 2023), the mean trustworthiness score for right-leaning sources is 26.7 (SD=23.4), in contrast to 61.7 (SD=20.1) for left-leaning sources (Mdiff=35.0, t(2978)=32.4, p < .001). Before the political orientation label changed from four to two categories, left- and right-leaning sources had moderate trustworthiness ratings (for example, in June 2021, 82.16 and 61.9, respectively) in contrast to far-left and far-right sources. In August/September 2021, however, when a high number of untrustworthy sources were added to the database, particularly the trustworthiness of slightly right-leaning sources dropped by almost 23 points, see Figure~\ref{fig:fig6}. When the label became binary, this led to a lower average score of right-leaning sources by adding low-quality sources classified as "far-right" before (where trustworthiness ranged between 13.5 and 17.8 points).

\textbf{Topics.} The database assigns topic labels based on the topics covered by the outlets. A source is usually assigned to multiple topics. The topic ratings were introduced around October 2020 and are incomplete for older versions of the database. In the most recent version of the database, only 161 sources are without a topic label (1.7\%). The most popular topics are ``Local News'' (39.1\%), ``Political News or Commentary'' (37.9\%), ``General News'' (19.9\%), ``Health or medical information'' (10.5\%), and ``Conspiracy Theories or Hoaxes'' (9.5\%). 

Up until 2022, fewer topic labels were given (see Fig.~\ref{fig:figa8}), but throughout the covered time frame, the most popular topics remained the same. Especially health-related information and political news peak at least once a year, most likely coinciding with update intervals. Interestingly, only about a year after the COVID-19 pandemic started, in August 2021, 1,039 labels related to COVID-19 misinformation were added to the database. This update coincides with a drop in trustworthiness for right-wing sources, as described above.

Trustworthiness ratings greatly differ across topics. Sources covering issues related to ``Military and Defense'' (M=58.3, SD=39.6) and ``Political news or commentary'' have the lowest average rating (M=61.5, SD=30.9), while those covering sports, business, or technology topics have the highest average trustworthiness scores (around 80). The quality of sources linked to each topic is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fig8}, with ratings from the most recent version of the database.

\section{Conclusion}
An inherent bias in misinformation research lies in the definitions of false information and its measurement methodologies. This review first describes source- and story-based approaches for tracking online misinformation and assesses their reliability, validity, and feasibility. Second, we describe the NewsGuard database, a prominent but expensive resource containing trustworthiness ratings for 10,658 sources, and discuss its potential shortcomings and strengths, as well as those of source-based approaches in general. 
Overall, the database extensively covers sources based in the United States, with lower coverage of the other 8 countries. Still, it is regularly updated across all nine countries. NewsGuard contains more right-wing sources than left-wing ones, with the former generally rated as less trustworthy. We discuss the implications of these findings and provide recommendations for utilizing the NewsGuard database across different contexts.

Examining the volatility of trustworthiness ratings, we find that over half of the rated news is considered trustworthy. Changes in source coverage can be abrupt, requiring that researchers carefully assess several database snapshots for consistency before choosing which one to use. Finding out which changes were made between updates is difficult as NewsGuard has not reported if they update periodically by country, after specific events, by domain, or by parent domain. Our findings suggest that trustworthiness ratings are regularly updated per country but that changes happen only infrequently and typically upon changes in website ownership or editorial practices (e.g., how sources label advertising or handle errors). Even then, changes in trustworthiness ratings are usually trivial. Depending on the granularity of the research objective, using a minimum of one snapshot per year should be efficient to cover major changes. However, we advise researchers to at least compare monthly snapshots of the database for major changes in coverage. Despite these nuances, the trustworthiness ratings by NewsGuard appear relatively insensitive to time, speaking for the reliability of source-based approaches. 

Furthermore, we compared ratings across countries and considered alternative lists to evaluate the validity of the NewsGuard database, particularly outside the U.S. context. We found that country-specific factors significantly influence coverage, with disparities evident among countries sharing similar media systems. While the coverage for English-speaking countries (U.S. and U.K.) is extensive, coverage for countries such as Italy, France, and Germany varies. Even though the countries covered have similar media systems, the journalistic criteria may not be applicable to all, as the distinct patterns in Italy and France in contrast to the other countries suggest. According to Brüggemann et al. \citeyear{bruggemannHallinManciniRevisited2014b} analysis of media systems, Italy and France belong to the Southern cluster that has a less professionalized journalism sector than the Central (e.g., Germany) or Western clusters (e.g., US). According to Hallin \& Mancini (2004), in their seminal definition of media systems, those in countries that fail to meet the professionalism indicators lack an understanding of serving the public interest. In other words, when journalistic professionalism is low, journalists lack professional autonomy and often serve economic or political interests. This may explain the lack of transparency as indicated by NewsGuards' journalistic criteria. The criteria that NewsGuard uses to judge the trustworthiness of sources thus seem to broadly reflect differences in media systems––at least for the Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic countries covered. Nonetheless, the discrepancy between Southern and other countries underscores that applications of the database need to be tailored to individual contexts.

In addition, we checked if other characteristics available in the NewsGuard database could be used for misinformation research. Exploring the political orientation of sources, we noticed a predominance of right-wing sources, raising questions about whether this disparity results from a sampling bias of NewsGuard editors rather than a bias on the supply side. Research on media biases suggests a prevalence of left-leaning opinions in major mainstream sources \citep{osmundsenPartisanPolarizationPrimary2021}, so smaller but more numerous right-leaning sources can be expected to emerge. This would also explain lower trustworthiness on the right-wing spectrum, which is in line with the political asymmetry repeatedly observed in susceptibility to misinformation, e.g., recently by Robertson et al. \citeyear{robertsonUsersChooseEngage2023a}. NewsGuard also discusses this topic in their 2023 Social Impact Report\footnote{https://web.archive.org/web/20240228003711/https://www.newsguardtech.com/special-reports/social-impact-report-2023/}, emphasizing that their ratings are intended to be apolitical. Yet, when sampling based on the NewsGuard list and interpreting the prevalence of misinformation across political spectra, this political bias may reproduce in downstream research results. Overall, political orientation ratings, especially outside the U.S., should be interpreted with caution due to potential incompleteness and sampling biases. 

Lastly, news topic and journalistic criteria ratings enrich source characterization and offer valuable insights into misinformation dynamics. %anything else to say about the topics?

Limitations of our analysis include that we only describe the database internally and over time, but not .... This is due to the scarcity of comparable datasets, especially for countries other than the United States and Germany.

In conclusion, while the NewsGuard database is a valuable tool for tracking online misinformation, researchers should weigh its utility against associated costs and consider alternative sources (e.g. \citep{linHighLevelCorrespondence2023} for comprehensive analysis. A promising approach was tested in a recent study by Yang and Menczer \citeyear{yangLargeLanguageModels2023a}, who compared NewsGuard ratings to assessments made by large language models. Although the latter showed somewhat lower performance, they serve as a more cost-effective starting point for assessing trustworthiness at the source level.
